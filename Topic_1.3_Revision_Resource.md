## SECTION 0 — FOUNDATIONAL DISTINCTIONS

### Distinction — A Priori vs A Posteriori Knowledge

**A priori:** Knowledge of a proposition is a priori if establishing its truth does not require sense experience (e.g. "Bachelors are unmarried"; "537 + 654 = 1191").

**A posteriori:** Knowledge whose truth can only be established through sense experience (e.g. "There are more than 6 billion people on Earth").

**The key difference:** It is not about how you acquired the concepts — it is about how you *check* whether the proposition is true. You needed sense experience to learn English, but you don't need it to verify "bachelors are unmarried."

**Common confusion to avoid:** Students conflate this with the analytic/synthetic distinction. They are different distinctions: a priori/a posteriori is *epistemological* (how we know), analytic/synthetic is *semantic* (what makes it true). Whether they line up is itself a contested philosophical question.

---

### Distinction — Analytic vs Synthetic Propositions

**Analytic:** A proposition is analytic if it is true (or false) just in virtue of the meanings of the words (e.g. "squares have four sides").

**Synthetic:** A proposition is synthetic if it is not analytic — true or false in virtue of the way the world is, not the meanings of words alone (e.g. "ripe tomatoes are red").

**The key difference:** Analytic truths can be known just by understanding the concepts; synthetic truths require something beyond mere understanding to verify.

**Common confusion to avoid:** Not every analytic truth is *obvious* — some require unpacking ("In five days' time, it will have been a week since the day which was tomorrow three days ago"). Obviousness is irrelevant to analyticity.

---

### Distinction — Necessary vs Contingent Truth

**Contingent:** A proposition is contingently true if it could have been false; the world could have been otherwise (e.g. "There are more types of insect than any other animal").

**Necessary:** A proposition is necessarily true if it must be true and could not have been false (e.g. "2 + 2 = 4"; analytic truths).

**The key difference:** Contingent = depends on how the world happens to be. Necessary = could not have been otherwise, holds across all possible worlds.

**Common confusion to avoid:** Don't confuse the *figure* "2" possibly meaning the number 3 with the *truth* "2 + 2 = 4" being contingent. Keep meanings fixed; under fixed meanings, "2 + 2 = 4" must hold.

---

### How the Distinctions Connect — Leibniz's Pivotal Argument

[Argument: Knowledge of Necessary Truths Must Be A Priori] — Leibniz

* P1: Sense experience only ever provides information about particular instances (these two apples and these two apples make four apples).
* P2: However many instances confirm a general truth, they cannot show that it is *necessarily* true.
* C1: Therefore experience cannot establish necessary truths.
* C2: Therefore knowledge of necessary truths must be a priori.

**Weakest premise:** P2. An empiricist might respond that "necessary" truths are really just analytic truths, knowable through conceptual analysis (which is a form of experience-grounded conceptual mastery), so a priori does not entail innate or rationally intuited.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does Leibniz's argument show that knowledge of necessary truths is *innate*, or only that it is a priori? (Answer: only a priori — the further step to innateness is separate.)

## SECTION 1 — RATIONALISM, EMPIRICISM, INNATISM

### Theory — Innatism (about knowledge)

Core claim: We have at least some propositional knowledge that is part of the structure of the mind from birth, not derived from sense experience.

What this commits the thinker to: There must be innate *concepts* (since propositional knowledge is structured by concepts).

What this rules out: That all knowledge is a posteriori; that the mind starts as a tabula rasa.

Key terminology:
- **Innate** — not gained from experience; built into the faculty of reason. Note: defenders allow that experience may *trigger* innate knowledge, but experience is necessary, not sufficient.

---

### Theory — Rationalism (refined: Rationalism 2.0)

Core claim: We have some a priori knowledge of synthetic propositions about the world *external to our minds*, gained by rational insight (intuition) and reasoning (deduction).

What this commits the thinker to: That reason can yield substantive truths about reality, not just truths-by-meaning. That intuition and deduction are genuine sources of knowledge.

What this rules out: The view that all a priori knowledge is analytic or merely about our own mental states.

Key terminology:
- **Rational intuition** — discovering the truth of a claim just by thinking about it; grasping that something must be true.
- **Deduction** — inference where the premises (if true) entail the conclusion.

---

### Theory — Empiricism (refined: Empiricism 2.0)

Core claim: There is no a priori knowledge of synthetic propositions about the world external to our minds (whether innate or from rational intuition and deduction). All such knowledge derives from experience.

What this commits the thinker to: Necessary truths are analytic. Concepts ultimately derive from sensation or reflection. The mind is (initially) a tabula rasa.

What this rules out: Innatism; classical rationalism.

Key terminology:
- **Tabula rasa** — Latin for "blank slate"; Locke's image of the mind prior to experience.
- **Sensation/Reflection** — Locke's two sources of all ideas: external (senses) and internal (introspection of mental operations).

## SECTION 2 — ARGUMENTS FOR INNATE KNOWLEDGE

### [Argument: The Slave Boy] — Plato (Meno)

* P1: The slave boy correctly answers a series of geometrical questions about doubling the area of a square, without ever having been taught geometry.
* P2: He cannot have gained this knowledge from experience (he wasn't taught; Socrates only asked questions, never told him the answer).
* P3: If he didn't gain it from experience, he must have recovered it from within his mind.
* C: Therefore the knowledge is innate (and learning is a form of recollection).

**Weakest premise:** P2. The boy could have gained the knowledge by *reasoning* prompted by Socrates' questions (as opposed to remembering it). Socrates' questions are not innocent — they structure the boy's reasoning in a way that arguably *teaches* him by guiding inference.

**Examiner's attack question:** How does Plato distinguish "recovering innate knowledge" from "reasoning one's way to a new conclusion"? If he cannot, the example collapses into ordinary inferential learning.

---

### [Argument: Knowledge of Necessary Truths Is Innate] — Leibniz (New Essays)

* P1: Knowledge of necessary truths (e.g. "It is impossible for the same thing to be and not to be") is a priori (from the argument above — experience can only teach how things *are*, not how they *must* be).
* P2: We discover this a priori knowledge by "attending carefully and methodically to what is already in our minds."
* P3: If we discover it by attending to what is already in the mind, it must be already in the mind.
* C: Therefore knowledge of necessary truths is innate.

Note: Leibniz allows experience is *necessary* for us to access this knowledge (we need words, sounds, prompts), but not *sufficient*. Sense experience triggers but does not produce the knowledge.

**Weakest premise:** P3. The empiricist responds that we may discover necessary truths by reflecting on concepts that we acquired from experience — so attending to "what is in our minds" doesn't mean attending to something innate; it could mean conceptual analysis of acquired concepts.

**Examiner's attack question:** How does Leibniz distinguish (a) innate knowledge accessed through reflection from (b) analytic knowledge derived from concepts learned via experience?

## SECTION 3 — LOCKE'S ATTACK ON INNATE KNOWLEDGE

### [Argument: No Innate Knowledge] — Locke (Essay, Bk 1, Ch. 2)

* P1: If there is innate knowledge, it must be universal — every human being must have it.
* P2: For an idea to be in the mind, the mind must be (or have been) conscious of it.
* C1: Therefore innate knowledge is knowledge that every human being is or has been conscious of.
* P3: Children and people with severe learning disabilities ("idiots") are not conscious of the standard candidates (e.g. "It is impossible for the same thing to be and not to be"; theorems in geometry) — they don't even understand them.
* C2: Therefore these claims are not innate.
* P4: There are no candidate truths universally consciously accepted by every human being including children.
* C3: Therefore there is no innate knowledge.

**Weakest premise:** P2 (the consciousness requirement). Defenders of innatism (Plato, Leibniz, Descartes) never claimed innate knowledge was *consciously* present from birth. Locke's whole argument turns on a definition his opponents reject.

**Examiner's attack question:** Why should an innatist accept Locke's definition of innate knowledge as conscious? Doesn't memory show that knowledge can exist unconsciously in the mind?

---

### Locke's Four Alternative Definitions of "Innate" — and his rejection of each

[Distinction: Locke's targets]

**Definition 1: "Innate" = anything we *can* come to know.**
Locke's reply: This is a misuse of the term — under this definition everything we ever learn would count as innate. What is innate, properly, is the *capacity* for knowledge, not the knowledge itself.

**Definition 2: "Innate" = what everyone assents to upon gaining the use of reason.**
Locke's reply: (i) If we already had the knowledge innately, why do we need to *discover* it via reasoning? (ii) Children can reason before they grasp logical/mathematical truths, so the timing fails.

**Definition 3: "Innate" = what is gained at some point after the use of reason.**
Locke's reply: This is hopeless — it doesn't distinguish innate knowledge from any other knowledge gained later in life.

**Definition 4: "Innate" = self-evident truths assented to as soon as understood.**
Locke's reply: Many such truths plainly depend on experience (e.g. "white is not black" requires experience of white and black), so they cannot be innate.

The key difference between these and Locke's own conception: Locke insists innate knowledge be *consciously* possessed and *universally* held. Innatists deny the consciousness condition.

Common confusion to avoid: Students often think Locke's argument is just "babies don't know logic." It is much more careful — he is testing every plausible definition of "innate" and showing that none of them yields any genuine examples.

## SECTION 4 — LEIBNIZ vs LOCKE ON INNATE KNOWLEDGE

### Locke vs Leibniz — Innate Knowledge

**Locke's position:** No knowledge is innate. Ideas exist in the mind only if the mind is conscious of them. The mind is a tabula rasa; all ideas come from sensation or reflection.

**Leibniz's position:** Some knowledge is innate, but as a *disposition*, *aptitude*, or *predisposition* — not as fully formed conscious thoughts. Memory and unconscious knowledge show that the mind can contain what it is not currently conscious of.

**The crux of the disagreement:** Whether ideas can exist in the mind without being conscious. Locke's P2 (the consciousness requirement) is the load-bearing premise; Leibniz attacks it directly with two moves:
1. We use the principle of non-contradiction constantly to distinguish concepts but without explicitly attending to it — this is unconscious knowledge in active use.
2. Memory shows knowledge can be unconscious between recollections; why must unconscious knowledge always have once been conscious?

**Who has the stronger argument and why:** Leibniz has the better of the conceptual point — Locke's definition is unfair to actual innatists. But Leibniz's positive account (innate knowledge as "veins in marble" disposing the sculptor's work) is metaphorical and underspecified. The dispute survives partly because the modern "triggering" model (e.g. Carruthers) gives Leibniz's view a more defensible empirical face — innate knowledge is genetically encoded but environment-triggered.

---

### Thinking Harder — Experience as "Trigger" (modern innatism)

Theory — Triggering Model of Innate Knowledge — Carruthers

Core claim: Innate knowledge is knowledge our cognitive capacities are *predisposed* to develop under appropriate environmental stimulation; experience triggers it but does not supply its content.

What this commits the thinker to: A genetic/evolutionary explanation of cognitive development (e.g. infants developing object permanence around 3–4 months; theory of mind; species-specific birdsong recognition).

What this rules out: The simple Lockean dichotomy of "either learned from experience or had consciously from birth." Triggering is a third option: experience necessary but insufficient.

Key terminology:
- **Triggering** — environmental input releases or activates an innately specified cognitive structure (the bird hears a snippet and "knows" the rest of the song).
- **Predisposition** — minds are not just *capable* of forming a given concept; they are *preshaped* to form just that concept and not others.

Note: This makes innatism arguably *compatible* with the spirit of empiricism (knowledge ultimately tracks evolved fit with the world), but only for *contingent* truths. Necessary truths still need a separate empiricist account (analyticity).

---

## SECTION 5 — INNATE CONCEPTS

### [Argument: No Innate Concepts] — Locke (Essay, Bk 1, Ch. 4)

* P1: Innate knowledge requires innate concepts (propositional knowledge is structured by concepts).
* P2: Whatever concepts we have, we are conscious of.
* P3: Innate concepts must be universal.
* C1: Therefore innate concepts must be concepts every human being is or has been conscious of.
* P4: Newborn babies have no concepts beyond perhaps WARMTH and PAIN — certainly not IDENTITY, IMPOSSIBILITY, or GOD.
* P5: Some societies historically lack the concept GOD; it is taught by teachers, not innate.
* P6: Any concept in the mind is either *new* (arriving from a fresh impression) or *remembered* (previously conscious). Innate concepts are supposed to be neither — which is impossible.
* C2: Therefore there are no innate concepts.

**Weakest premise:** P2 (again, the consciousness requirement) and P6 (the new-or-remembered exhaustion). Both presuppose Locke's controversial conception of mental contents.

**Examiner's attack question:** Why should we accept that "in the mind" entails "conscious of"? Why couldn't a concept exist as a disposition to think in a particular way, neither new nor remembered?

---

### Leibniz's Defence of Innate Concepts (New Essays, Bk 1, Ch. 3)

[Topic of Dispute: Are concepts like IDENTITY, IMPOSSIBILITY, GOD, SUBSTANCE innate?]

The objection (Locke): Babies plainly lack these concepts; whole atheist societies lack GOD; there is no third category of mental content beyond new-or-remembered.

The response (Leibniz):
1. To Locke's first objection: yes babies don't yet have IDENTITY etc., but they have the *disposition* to form them. They are essential to all thought; it just takes time to make them explicit.
2. To Locke's second objection: lacking the *word* for God is not the same as lacking the *concept* of GOD. Reflection on nature universally disposes minds to form a concept of a higher power; the full concept emerges through development.
3. To Locke's third objection: Innate concepts are neither new nor remembered — they are *dispositional*. As a sculptor finds a shape already outlined in the veins of the marble, so the mind finds in itself what experience helps it uncover.

Who has the stronger position and why: Leibniz answers Locke convincingly *on Leibniz's own terms*. But the empirical evidence cuts both ways: Locke is right that some societies lack a developed God-concept (challenging universality), while Leibniz is right that disposition-based innateness is conceptually coherent. The dispute moves from "is innateness possible?" (Leibniz wins) to "is there positive evidence for it?" (this is empirical and ongoing — modern cognitive science supports innate predispositions for some content but not others).

## SECTION 6 — THE EMPIRICIST THEORY OF CONCEPTS

### Theory — Locke's Two Sources of Ideas (Tabula Rasa Thesis)

Core claim: At birth the mind is a blank slate. All ideas derive from one of two sources: (1) **sensation** (perception of external objects) and (2) **reflection** (introspection of the mind's own operations — perception, thinking, willing).

What this commits the thinker to: The denial of innate ideas/concepts; the view that even abstract concepts must be traceable to experience.

What this rules out: Concepts that cannot be analysed into components from sensation or reflection.

Key terminology:
- **Sensation** — ideas of "sensible qualities" derived from sense perception.
- **Reflection** — internal perception of one's own mental operations (introspection).
- **Idea** — Locke's broad term covering sensations, concepts, and propositional thoughts.

---

### Distinction — Hume's Impressions vs Ideas

**Impression:** A direct, forceful, vivid perception (sensing or feeling). Subdivided into impressions of *sensation* (from senses) and *reflection* (from inner experience including emotions).

**Idea:** A fainter copy of an impression (thinking, remembering, imagining).

**The key difference (three criteria Hume gives):**
1. *Forcefulness/vivacity* — impressions are more vivid than ideas.
2. *Copying* — ideas are copies of impressions (this is the foundational claim).
3. *Liability to confusion* — we make mistakes about ideas more easily than about impressions.

**Common confusion to avoid:** Hume's "ideas" are NOT the same as Locke's "ideas." Hume's ideas correspond roughly to *concepts* and to the contents of imagination/memory; impressions are the live perceptual/emotional events.

---

### Theory — Hume's Copy Principle

Core claim: All simple ideas are (fainter) copies of corresponding simple impressions; therefore all our concepts ultimately derive from experience.

What this commits the thinker to: That for any genuine concept, there must be an originating impression. Where no such impression exists, the supposed concept is empty or confused (the basis of Hume's metaphysics-cleanser).

What this rules out: Innate concepts; substantive metaphysical concepts whose impressions cannot be located.

Key terminology:
- **Simple concept/impression** — undivided, uniform: a single colour, smell, shape (e.g. RED).
- **Complex concept** — built up from simple concepts by combination, abstraction, or original recombination (e.g. UNICORN = HORSE + HORN + WHITENESS).

---

### Two Hume Arguments for the Copy Principle

[Argument: Argument from Lacking Experience]
* P1: Without experience of a particular kind, a person cannot form the relevant idea (a blind man cannot conceive colour; a mild person cannot grasp the motive of revenge).
* P2: This is best explained by ideas being copied from impressions.
* C: Therefore all ideas derive from impressions.

[Argument: Argument from Simple/Complex Analysis]
* P1: All concepts are either simple or complex.
* P2: All complex concepts can be analysed into simple concepts.
* P3: All simple concepts can be matched to a simple impression.
* C: Therefore all concepts ultimately derive from experience.

**Weakest premise:** P3 in the second argument — Hume himself admits a counterexample (the missing shade of blue), discussed below.

**Examiner's attack question:** If even one simple idea (the missing shade of blue) can be formed without a corresponding impression, why couldn't other ideas be formed similarly?

## SECTION 7 — ISSUES WITH THE EMPIRICIST THEORY OF CONCEPTS

### [Argument: The Missing Shade of Blue] — Hume (against his own theory, dismissed by Hume but pressed by critics)

* P1: Present someone with a spectrum of blues with one shade missing.
* P2: They can imagine the missing shade without ever having had an impression of it.
* C: Therefore it is possible to form an idea not copied from any impression.

Hume's two possible rescues:

(1) **Weakened copy principle:** Ideas are meaningful provided they *could* have been copied from an impression. The missing shade meets this — metaphysical ideas like SUBSTANCE arguably do not.

(2) **Structural exception:** Where simple impressions form an *ordered series* (shades from dark to light), the mind can fill gaps by interpolation. This works only when impressions are tightly resemblance-structured; a blind man has no analogous series for colour.

**Weakest premise:** Either rescue weakens the copy principle. The worry: if it can be weakened to allow this case, what principled reason rules out other apparent counterexamples (SUBSTANCE, GOD, KNOWLEDGE)?

**Examiner's attack question:** Does the missing shade of blue undermine Hume's whole methodological use of the copy principle as a metaphysical razor?

---

### Leibniz on "Intellectual Ideas" — Concepts from Reflection are Innate

Theory — Reflection-Yields-Innate-Concepts — Leibniz

Core claim: Concepts derived from reflection (BEING, UNITY, SUBSTANCE, DURATION, CHANGE, ACTION, PERCEPTION, PLEASURE) are innate, because to reflect is to attend to what is already within us.

What this commits the thinker to: That the mind is its own object of cognition in a way that does not constitute experience-of-the-external in the relevant empiricist sense.

What this rules out: That all concepts trace to sensation; the strict tabula rasa thesis.

Key terminology:
- **Intellectual idea** — Leibniz's term for ideas of reflection.

### [Topic of Dispute: Are Reflection-Concepts Innate?]

The objection (Locke, on Leibniz's behalf in the textbook's reconstruction): A confusion. The fact that I am a substance, that I perceive, etc., does not entail that the *concepts* SUBSTANCE and PERCEPTION are innate. I had to *experience* my own mental activity (in reflection) before I could form the relevant concepts — so the concepts come from experience, not from innateness. Reflection IS a kind of experience.

The response (Leibniz): To reflect is to attend to what is already within us, and "something that we carry with us already is not something that came from the senses." Reflection is therefore not experience in the empiricist sense; it is the mind's apprehension of its own innate structure.

Who has the stronger position and why: Locke wins on most reflection-concepts (SUBSTANCE, PERCEPTION, etc.) — these can plausibly be derived from introspective experience. But there is a residue Leibniz can fall back on: concepts that *cannot* be explained even by reflection, such as IMPOSSIBILITY or SQUARE — because I am not impossible and I am not a square. For these, the reflection-route is unavailable, and the choice is again between innateness, analyticity, or confusion. Locke's strategy here is to argue these are not concepts in their own right but constructions from simple experiential concepts; whether this is viable is contested.

**Key worry for Leibniz:** Even if the reflection-route works for SUBSTANCE, it cannot explain concepts like IMPOSSIBILITY or SQUARE. So another explanation is needed for these.

---

### [Topic of Dispute: PHYSICAL SUBSTANCE]

The objection (Hume / Berkeley): The concept PHYSICAL SUBSTANCE is the concept of something independent of experience existing in three-dimensional space. But experience cannot show us something that exists *independently* of experience. We never perceive the desk when we don't perceive it.

The response (Hume's positive account): The concept arises from a *confusion*, not from impressions and not from innate sources. Two genuine experiential resources combine:
- *Similarity*: my experience-of-desk now is qualitatively very like my earlier experience-of-desk.
- *Unity*: in any one experience I can experience an object as numerically one.

The imagination then conflates qualitative similarity with numerical identity — generating the (confused) idea of a thing that persists between experiences.

Who has the stronger position and why: Hume's diagnosis is elegant but radical — it commits him to saying our common-sense concept of physical objects is *confused*, which is hard to swallow. The innatist counter-move (used by Leibniz/Descartes, see Trademark) is: if the concept can't come from experience and isn't confused, it must be innate. The disagreement reveals a trilemma — concepts of substance must be (a) experiential, (b) innate, or (c) confused — and there is no comfortable winner.

---

### [Topic of Dispute: MENTAL SUBSTANCE]

The objection (Hume): We don't experience a continuing self over time, only "a continually changing array of thoughts and feelings." There is no impression of a unified mental substance; therefore no idea of one — at most the same similarity-mistaken-for-identity confusion.

The response (Berkeley/Descartes): I am directly aware of my mind not just as activity but as the *subject* that is active in thinking, perceiving, willing. So the concept MIND derives from genuine reflective experience.

Who has the stronger position and why: Hume's argument bites only if his radically atomistic phenomenology is correct. Descartes' cogito (next section) is the strongest counter-move: I can't even formulate the doubt without presupposing a thinker. But Descartes admits this only delivers "I think" while it is being thought — extending it to a *persisting* substance is the real battleground.

## SECTION 8 — THE INTUITION AND DEDUCTION THESIS

### Distinction — Intuition vs Deduction

**Rational intuition (insight):** Discovering the truth of a claim just by thinking about it; immediate grasping of why something must be true (e.g. seeing that 2 + 2 = 4 *must* be true; seeing why the conclusion follows from the premises in a valid argument).

**Deduction:** A form of reasoning where the premises (if true) entail the conclusion. Validity: it is impossible for premises to be true and conclusion false.

**The key difference:** Intuition is direct, immediate apprehension of truth. Deduction is mediated inferential extension from intuited premises to further conclusions.

**Common confusion to avoid:** "Intuition" here does NOT mean gut feeling, hunch, or instinct. It is *rational* intuition — careful judgement that something must be the case.

---

### Theory — The Intuition and Deduction Thesis — Descartes

Core claim: We can gain a priori knowledge of substantive synthetic truths about the world (including the existence of God and of physical objects) by (i) intuiting clear and distinct premises and (ii) deductively inferring further truths from them.

What this commits the thinker to: That at least some clear and distinct ideas are reliably true; that reason has cognitive reach beyond mere conceptual analysis.

What this rules out: The empiricist limitation of a priori knowledge to analytic truths and truths about our own minds.

Key terminology:
- **Clear idea** — present and accessible to the attentive mind (analogous to seeing something clearly with sufficient strength).
- **Distinct idea** — clear *and* sharply separated from all other ideas, with every part of it clear.
- **The natural light** — Descartes' name for our faculty of rational intuition that recognises clear and distinct truth.

---

### Theory — Hume's Fork

Core claim: All knowable propositions divide exhaustively into (a) **relations of ideas** and (b) **matters of fact**.

What this commits the thinker to:
- *Relations of ideas* are knowable a priori, intuitively or demonstratively certain, deniable only on pain of contradiction (e.g. 3 × 5 = 30 ÷ 2). They concern only how concepts relate.
- *Matters of fact* are about what exists; they can be denied without contradiction; they are knowable only a posteriori, ultimately via causal inference grounded in experience.

What this rules out: Substantive a priori knowledge of synthetic propositions about the external world. Everything Descartes claims to know by intuition and deduction must be either analytic (= relation of ideas, hence trivial-in-Hume's-sense) or empirical.

Key terminology:
- **Causal inference** — the foundation of all knowledge of matters of fact beyond present experience and memory; not a priori but learned from experience.

**The four empiricist responses to any rationalist claim of intuited synthetic truth:**
1. The proposition is analytic, not synthetic.
2. The proposition concerns the speaker's own mind (known via reflection).
3. The proposition is known a posteriori, not a priori.
4. The proposition isn't known at all.

## SECTION 9 — THE COGITO

### [Argument: The Cogito] — Descartes (Meditation II)

* P1: I am being deceived by an evil demon about everything I can be deceived about (the methodic doubt).
* P2: To be deceived I must exist (the demon cannot make me into nothing while I think I am something).
* P3: Doubting is a form of thinking.
* P4: I cannot doubt that I think (any attempt to doubt confirms it).
* C: Therefore I exist as a thinking thing.

Descartes adds: I know I exist *whatever* my body is or isn't — even if I have no body, I still know I think. So my essence (as far as I can know certainly) is *thinking*, not body-having.

**Weakest premise:** The implicit slide from "thinking is occurring" to "I (a persistent thinking *thing*, a substance) exist." Hume's challenge bites here: why a *thing* rather than just a series of thoughts?

**Examiner's attack question:** Even granting that thinking requires a thinker, does this give Descartes a *persistent* mental substance, or only a thinker for *this* thought?

---

### [Argument: Clear and Distinct Ideas as a General Rule] — Descartes (Meditation III)

* P1: My certainty in the cogito rests on its being clearly and distinctly perceived.
* P2: If clarity and distinctness do not guarantee truth, I cannot know that I exist.
* P3: I do know that I exist.
* C: Therefore as a general rule, whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive is true.

**Weakest premise:** The generalisation step from one case (the cogito) to a *general* criterion. Why think what works for "I exist" works for everything else clear and distinct? Also: is "clear and distinct" *psychological* (felt indubitability) or *epistemic* (necessarily-true-when-attended-to)? Descartes insists on the latter, but critics suspect he equivocates.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does Descartes' rule that clear and distinct ideas are true depend on God's existence — but his proof of God's existence depends on the rule? (The "Cartesian Circle.")

---

### [Topic of Dispute: The Cartesian Circle]

The objection (Arnauld and other contemporaries; standard textbook objection): Descartes appears to argue in a circle.

The structure of the alleged circle in numbered form:
* (a) Descartes uses *clear and distinct ideas* as the basis for proving that God exists (in the Trademark, cosmological, and ontological arguments).
* (b) But he also says we can only be *certain* that clear and distinct ideas are true once we know that God exists and is non-deceiving.
* C: Therefore he cannot prove God's existence (which requires reliance on clear and distinct ideas) without already having proved God's existence (to validate clear and distinct ideas). The argument is circular.

The response (Descartes): Distinguish two epistemic states:
1. *While I am attending to* a clear and distinct idea, I cannot doubt it — at that moment its truth is forced upon me.
2. *When I am no longer attending to it*, I can doubt whether what I previously found clear and distinct really was true (perhaps my faculties are unreliable).

So Descartes' claim is: I can prove God's existence using clear and distinct ideas *while attending to them* (no circularity at this stage). What God's existence then secures is the *retrospective* reliability of clearly-and-distinctly-perceived premises — i.e., that when I remember having clearly perceived something, I can trust the memory. God doesn't make clear-and-distinct ideas true; he secures the bridge from "perceived clearly at time t" to "still known at time t+1."

Who has the stronger position and why: This response is widely judged inadequate. The objector can press: even *while attending* to a clear and distinct idea, I might be the kind of thinker for whom clarity-and-distinctness is *not* a reliable mark of truth (the evil demon could have made me such). I cannot rule this out without proving God exists — but proving God exists requires accepting clear-and-distinct ideas as truth-tracking. The circle reappears one level up: I need a guarantee of the *truth-conduciveness* of clarity-and-distinctness, not just a guarantee that I cannot help believing them while attending. Most commentators conclude the circle stands.

**Examiner's attack question:** Even if we grant Descartes' "while attending" / "after attending" distinction, does it really escape the circle? Couldn't an evil demon make a thinker for whom even careful attention to clear-and-distinct ideas yields falsehoods?

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Does the Cogito Establish a Mental Substance?]

The objection (Hume): Even if thoughts logically require a thinker, they do not require *the same* thinker across different thoughts. Reflection finds only "a continually changing array of thoughts and feelings" — never a unified self. Descartes confuses similarity-of-mental-states with identity-of-substance. The evil demon could simply be creating a sequence of false thoughts, including the further thought "an 'I' exists."

The response (Descartes, "Objections and Replies"): Thoughts *logically* require a thinker; one cannot have thinking without a subject doing the thinking.

The counter (Hume): Granted thoughts require a thinker — but they don't require *one and the same* thinker across distinct thoughts. As soon as Descartes says that to be a thinker is to doubt, will, imagine etc., he assumes these activities belong to the same subject. That assumption is unwarranted.

The Berkeleyan position (an alternative empiricist view, importantly distinct from Hume's): We are aware of ourselves not just as bundles of mental activity but as the *active subject* that is thinking, perceiving, willing. So we *can* infer the existence of a mental substance — but from experience of ourselves, not from rational intuition of "clear and distinct ideas." This makes knowledge of the self a posteriori (from impressions of reflection), not a priori as Descartes claims.

The empiricist additional charge: Even if the cogito establishes something, it isn't established by *rational intuition*. It is known via impressions of reflection — i.e., a posteriori, not a priori. So the cogito is not the example of intuition-and-deduction Descartes claims it is.

Who has the stronger position and why: Hume's local point is sharp — for any *single* thought, "I exist" is established only for the duration of that thought. Descartes' move to a persisting substance smuggles in more than the methodic doubt licenses. But Descartes can fall back to a minimum claim: while I think, I exist as something thinking. That minimum is unchallengeable. The question is whether it gives him enough to build the rest of the system on.

## SECTION 10 — THE TRADEMARK ARGUMENT

### [Argument: The Trademark Argument] — Descartes (Meditation III)

* P1: I have the concept GOD (a being supremely powerful, infinite, perfect).
* P2: Every idea has a cause.
* P3: A cause must contain at least as much "reality" as its effect; the cause of an idea must have as much reality as what the idea represents.
* P4: As a finite, imperfect mind I can create ideas of things up to my own degree of reality — but not of things with *more* reality than me.
* P5: GOD is the concept of something with *infinitely more* reality than me.
* C1: Therefore I cannot have invented the concept GOD.
* P6: It does not derive from sense experience (it isn't an "unexpected" external impression).
* C2: Therefore the concept GOD is innate, stamped on my mind by its creator.
* C3: Therefore God exists.

**Weakest premise:** P3 (the causal-reality principle) and P4. Both depend on a medieval metaphysics of "degrees of reality" (substance > attribute > mode) that empiricists reject as unmotivated. Hume can attack via the fork: the principle "a cause must have as much reality as its effect" is neither analytic nor known from experience.

**Examiner's attack question:** How does Descartes know the causal-reality principle a priori? Isn't it a synthetic claim about causation that, by Hume's lights, must be established by experience (and clearly cannot be)?

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Origin of the Concept GOD]

The objection (Hume): The idea of God arises by "extending without limit the qualities of goodness and wisdom that we find in our own minds." Starting from finite goodness/wisdom, we negate finitude (NOT-FINITE = INFINITE) and imperfection (NOT-IMPERFECT = PERFECT). The concept is constructible from experience-given materials; no innateness required.

The response (Descartes): The idea of imperfection presupposes a positive idea of perfection — we can only recognise ourselves as imperfect by *contrast* with perfection, so the perfection-concept must come first. Likewise infinity: we can't first know the finite without an idea of the unlimited.

The empiricist counter-response (the "hazy concept" objection): Descartes' reply works for some concept-pairs but not others. It is intuitive for REAL/UNREAL (we experience the real, then construct UNREAL by negation; we cannot do this in reverse). But for FINITE/INFINITE and IMPERFECT/PERFECT, the dependence runs the *other way* — we plausibly first experience limits, then construct a new concept UNLIMITED by negation, then derive INFINITY and PERFECTION from it. Furthermore, PERFECTION and INFINITY (if they mean more than mere "not-imperfect" and "not-finite") are challenging and unclear concepts. Descartes claims our idea of God is positively, clearly, and distinctly that of an infinite and perfect being — yet he simultaneously admits that as a finite mind he cannot *grasp* this idea, only *understand* it. With this admission, his claim that GOD is *clear and distinct* (and therefore eligible for his rational-intuition method) is unpersuasive.

Who has the stronger position and why: Descartes' move works for REAL/UNREAL but is far weaker for FINITE/INFINITE. Worse, his concession that he cannot grasp the idea of God (only understand it) is in significant tension with his claim that the idea is clear and distinct — the very property the Trademark argument needs it to have. Hume has the better of this exchange.

---

### Empiricist Objections via Hume's Fork (against the Trademark argument)

[Argument: The Causal Principle Is Not Knowable A Priori] — Hume

* P1: "Everything has a cause" is not analytic — its denial is not a contradiction.
* P2: Therefore it cannot be established by intuition/deduction (Hume's fork).
* P3: It is also not established by experience — experience cannot demonstrate exceptionless universal claims.
* C: Therefore Descartes cannot know a priori that all ideas (including GOD) have a cause.

A further attack on the same lines: "A cause must contain as much reality as its effect" is also synthetic and not knowable a priori. Hume's fork closes off the route Descartes needs.

**Weakest premise:** P3 — even granting that experience can't yield strict universals, defenders can argue that universal causal regularity is the best explanation of our experience and so warranted as inductively grounded background.

**Examiner's attack question:** Can Descartes give *any* a priori grounding for the causal-reality principle that isn't merely an appeal to its being clear and distinct (which begs the question)?

---

## SECTION 11 — THE COSMOLOGICAL ARGUMENT

### [Argument: Cosmological Argument] — Descartes (Meditation III)

* P1: If I caused my own existence I would have given myself all perfections.
* P2: I do not have all perfections.
* C1: Therefore I am not the cause of my own existence.
* P3: A lifespan is composed of independent moments — existing now does not entail or cause existing later.
* P4: My existence is not uncaused.
* C2: Therefore some cause is needed to keep me in existence (moment to moment).
* P5: I do not have the power to sustain my own continued existence.
* C3: Therefore I depend on something else to exist.
* P6: I am a thinking thing with the idea of GOD.
* P7: A cause must have as much reality as its effect.
* C4: Therefore my cause must be a thinking thing with the idea of GOD.
* P8: Either that cause is self-caused or caused by another.
* P9: An infinite regress of causes is impossible.
* C5: Therefore some cause must be self-caused.
* P10: Only God is self-caused.
* C6: Therefore God exists.

(Subsidiary argument: my parents started but cannot *sustain* my existence; only God can; therefore God exists.)

**Weakest premise:** P3/P4 (the requirement of moment-to-moment sustaining cause) and P9 (no infinite regress). Both are hostage to Hume's fork: neither is analytic, neither is empirically demonstrated.

**Examiner's attack question:** Why must my continued existence have a sustaining cause distinct from my existence in the previous moment? What argument rules out an infinite regress as analytically impossible (rather than merely strange)?

---

### Empiricist Responses to the Cosmological Argument

[Argument: Hume's Fork against the Cosmological Argument]

* P1: The claim "everything has a cause" is not analytic and not empirically established.
* P2: The claim "an infinite regress of causes is impossible" is not analytic and not empirically established.
* P3: The claim "a cause must have as much reality as its effect" is not analytic and not empirically established.
* C: Therefore the central premises of Descartes' cosmological argument cannot be known a priori, and the argument fails as a piece of intuition-and-deduction.

**Weakest premise:** P2 has the most pull-back — defenders can argue that an actual infinite regress is metaphysically problematic. But Hume's point is just that we cannot *know* this a priori.

**Examiner's attack question:** Even granting Hume on causation, can we still give an inductive cosmological argument? (Descartes would reject this — it falls short of certainty — but his rationalist standards are themselves disputable.)

---

## SECTION 12 — THE ONTOLOGICAL ARGUMENT

### [Argument: Ontological Argument] — Descartes (Meditation V)

* P1: I have the idea of GOD.
* P2: The idea of GOD is the idea of a supremely perfect being.
* P3: A supremely perfect being lacks no perfection.
* P4: Existence is a perfection.
* C: Therefore God exists.

(Descartes' supplementary unpacking: a supremely perfect being is omnipotent. An omnipotent being cannot depend on another for existence (it would lack a power). Therefore an omnipotent being is self-existent, eternal — therefore God necessarily exists.)

**Weakest premise:** P4 (existence as a perfection / property). The argument equivocates between (a) what is *contained in the concept* GOD and (b) what *exists* in reality. Critics: existence is not a "perfection" alongside others; it is the precondition for having any properties at all.

**Examiner's attack question:** Can Descartes coherently treat existence as a property without conceding Hume's critique that "exists" assertions are synthetic and so deniable without contradiction?

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Can Existence Be Deduced from a Concept?]

The objection (Hume, Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion):
* P1: Whatever is distinctly conceivable does not imply a contradiction.
* P2: Whatever we conceive as existent we can also conceive as non-existent.
* C: There is no being whose non-existence implies a contradiction (so no being exists necessarily, a priori).

Equivalent framing: claims about existence are matters of fact — synthetic propositions — whose denial is never contradictory.

The response (Descartes): With finite minds we usually entertain divine perfections separately and so don't notice their entailments. Careful reflection shows that omnipotence, perfection, and necessary existence cannot be conceived apart. So denying God's existence *is* contradictory, even if not obviously so.

Who has the stronger position and why: Hume has the better of this exchange. Even granting that the divine perfections cluster together conceptually, the ontological argument still needs to move from *conceptual entailment among perfections* to *real-world existence* — and that gap is precisely what Hume's fork is designed to expose. Descartes is reduced to insisting his rational intuition is reliable; without independent grounding (which the Trademark/cosmological arguments don't provide without circularity), this is a stalemate at best.

---

## SECTION 13 — PROOF OF THE EXTERNAL WORLD

### [Argument: The Wax Argument] — Descartes (Meditation II)

Aim: To establish what we *think* a physical object is, and to show our concept of it is clear, distinct, and not derived from sensory imagery.

* P1: When I melt wax it loses all its original sensory qualities (taste, smell, feel, shape).
* P2: I judge it is the same wax.
* C1: Therefore what I think the wax *is* is not its sensory qualities.
* P3: What I think the wax is is what remains through the changes — something *extended* and *changeable* (capable of change in spatial properties).
* P4: The wax can undergo more possible changes than I can imagine.
* C2: Therefore my concept of the wax cannot derive from imagination (which works with images from sense experience).
* C3: Therefore I comprehend the wax by the *understanding* alone.
* C4: Only this intellectual conception, not the perceptual experience, is clear and distinct.

**Weakest premise:** P3/C3. An empiricist can grant that we abstract a concept of "extended changeable thing" *from* experience of qualities; we don't need a separate intellectual faculty independent of sense.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does the wax argument show our concept of physical object is innate, or only that it is abstract? Hume would say we abstract it from sensory impressions and add a confused element of substance.

---

### [Argument: Possibility of Physical Objects] — Descartes (Meditation VI)

* P1: I have a clear and distinct idea of what a physical object is.
* P2: God exists and is supremely powerful.
* P3: The only reason God cannot make something is if its concept is contradictory.
* C1: Therefore God can make physical objects.
* C2: Therefore (given God exists) physical objects are possible.

**Weakest premise:** Depends on the prior God-arguments succeeding.

---

### [Argument: From Imagination] — Descartes (Meditation VI)

* P1: Imagination uses images (we can imagine a triangle), but understanding works without images (we can understand a chiliagon — 1000-sided figure — without imagining it).
* P2: Imagining requires more effort than understanding.
* C1: Imagination and understanding are different faculties.
* P3: Imagination is not essential to me (I'd still be a thinking thing without it); understanding is.
* P4: The best explanation for these differences is that imagination depends on having a body.
* C2: Therefore it is *probable* that the body (physical object) exists.

Note: Descartes himself flags this as only probable, not certain — so it doesn't yet deliver knowledge.

**Weakest premise:** P4 (inference to best explanation is exactly the kind of inductive move Descartes' rationalist standards reject elsewhere; using it here looks ad hoc).

---

### [Argument: From Perception (the main proof)] — Descartes (Meditation VI)

* P1: I have involuntary perceptual experiences of physical objects.
* P2: These experiences are caused by some substance.
* P3: If the cause were my own mind, the experiences would be voluntary.
* P4: I would know if my experiences were voluntary (since I know my own mind).
* C1: Therefore the cause is not my own mind.
* C2: Therefore the cause is some substance outside me — either God (directly) or physical objects.
* P5: If God caused them directly, God has given me an uncorrectable strong tendency to a false belief (that physical objects exist).
* P6: Such deception would make God a deceiver.
* P7: God is perfect, hence not a deceiver.
* C3: Therefore God did not cause my experiences directly to deceive me.
* C4: Therefore physical objects exist and cause my perceptual experiences.

**Weakest premise:** P2 (everything has a cause — Hume's fork) and P4 (we have privileged self-knowledge — Hume's bundle theory of mind denies this). The whole edifice also rests on the prior God-arguments.

**Examiner's attack question:** Is Descartes' confidence that I would *know* if my perceptual experiences were voluntary itself a clear-and-distinct claim, or a piece of contestable introspective optimism?

---

### Empiricist Responses to the Proof of the External World

Three lines of attack:

(1) **Causal-principle attack** (Hume's fork): P2 (everything has a cause) is neither analytic nor empirically established. So the whole inferential machinery fails.

(2) **God-dependency attack:** The argument depends on God existing and being non-deceiving. If the Trademark/cosmological/ontological arguments fail, so does this one. (This is the most direct objection — pull out one Jenga block, the tower collapses.)

(3) **Inference-to-best-explanation alternative** (Locke/Russell): We don't need certainty. Inductive abductive arguments (the existence of mind-independent physical objects best explains the involuntariness, vivacity, and coherence of our experience) are sufficient for ordinary knowledge. Descartes' rejection of inductive routes to physical-object knowledge is over-demanding.

(4) **Berkeleyan attack:** The very concept of mind-*independent* physical substance is incoherent — neither deductive nor inductive arguments could establish what cannot coherently exist.

(5) **Direct-realist attack:** No proof of physical objects is needed — perception is an open awareness *of* physical objects from the start. Descartes' assumption that perceptions are sense-data (mental intermediaries) is the original mistake.

There is no positive evidence of pre-natal acquisition. | The questions only *prompt*; the boy supplies the answers, which means the knowledge was present in some form. The new-or-remembered exhaustion ignores dispositional possession. | Reflection itself is a form of experience — so even concepts of mind/substance derived from reflection are not innate. Locke would resist the dispositional move as making "innate" trivially true of anything we can come to know. |
