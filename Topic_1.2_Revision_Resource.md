## SECTION 0 — FRAMING DISTINCTIONS

### Distinction — Realism vs Anti-realism (Idealism) about Perception

**Realism:** Holds that what we perceive (or what causes our perceptions) are physical objects which exist mind-independently.

**Idealism (anti-realism):** Holds that what we perceive are mental things — ideas — and that physical objects do not exist independently of minds.

**The key difference:** Realism asserts mind-independent existence; idealism denies it. The dispute is partly metaphysical (what exists?) and partly epistemological (how do we know?).

**Common confusion to avoid:** "Direct realism" and "indirect realism" are *both* realisms — they agree physical objects exist mind-independently. They disagree only about whether we perceive them directly or via mediating sense-data.

---

### Distinction — Direct vs Indirect Perception

**Direct perception:** The immediate object of perception is the physical object itself; nothing mental stands between perceiver and object.

**Indirect perception:** The immediate object of perception is a mind-dependent sense-datum; the physical object is perceived via or in virtue of that sense-datum.

**The key difference:** What is the *immediate* (not eventual) object of awareness? For the direct realist, the world; for the indirect realist, sense-data caused by the world.

**Common confusion to avoid:** "Indirect" does not mean "inferred." Indirect realists need not say we *infer* physical objects from sense-data (some say sense-data are how we perceive physical objects, not what we perceive instead of them).

---

### Distinction — Veridical / Illusory / Hallucinatory Perception

**Veridical perception:** A perceptual experience that represents the world as it actually is.

**Illusion:** A perceptual experience in which a real physical object appears to have a property it does not actually have (e.g. a straight pencil looking crooked when half-submerged in water).

**Hallucination:** A perceptual experience in which there is no physical object being perceived at all (e.g. seeing a pink elephant that isn't there).

**The key difference:** In illusion, the object is there but misrepresented; in hallucination, no relevant object is there at all.

**Common confusion to avoid:** Illusions can be *subjectively indistinguishable* from veridical perception — and so can hallucinations. This subjective indistinguishability is the engine of the standard arguments against direct realism.

---

### Distinction — Sense-Data vs Physical Objects

**Sense-data:** Mind-dependent appearances; the immediate "content" of perceptual experience. Three defining features: (i) they are exactly as they seem (no appearance/reality gap); (ii) they only exist while being experienced; (iii) they are private (no two people share numerically the same sense-datum).

**Physical objects:** Mind-independent things; can appear differently from how they really are; exist when unperceived; are public (can be experienced by multiple people).

**The key difference:** Sense-data are essentially mind-dependent; physical objects are essentially mind-independent.

**Common confusion to avoid:** Don't conflate sense-data with the physical light-waves, sound-waves, or chemicals involved in perception. Light-waves are *physical*. Sense-data are *mental*.

## SECTION 1 — DIRECT REALISM

### Theory — Direct Realism

Core claim: The immediate objects of perception are mind-independent physical objects and their properties.

What this commits the thinker to: Physical objects exist objectively, in space and time, independent of being perceived. Perception is a form of "openness" to the world — direct awareness sensitive to how objects are.

What this rules out: Any mediating mental items (sense-data) standing between perceiver and physical object; any view on which we *infer* the existence of physical objects.

Key terminology:
- **Openness** — the direct realist's image of perception as putting us in immediate cognitive contact with mind-independent reality (sensitive to differences in how objects are).
- **Immediate object of perception** — the thing of which we are first/directly aware in perception.

---

### [Argument: Perceptual Variation] — Russell (Problems of Philosophy, Ch. 1)

* P1: There are variations in how a single physical object is perceived (e.g. the table looks different shades of brown depending on lighting and viewing angle; rectangular from 90° but obtuse from other angles; smooth to touch but craggy under a microscope).
* P2: Our perception varies without corresponding changes in the physical object.
* C1: Therefore the properties physical objects have and the properties they appear to have are not identical.
* C2: Therefore what we are immediately aware of in perception is not exactly the same as what exists independently of our minds.
* C3: Therefore we do not perceive physical objects directly. (We perceive sense-data.)

**Weakest premise:** The inference from C2 to C3. That what I am aware of differs from the object in some respect doesn't strictly entail there is a separate mental object I'm aware of *instead* — perhaps I perceive the object as having a relational appearance-property.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does perceptual variation show we perceive sense-data, or only that physical objects have *relational* properties (e.g. "looking obtuse-from-here") in addition to their intrinsic properties?

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Perceptual Variation]

The objection (Russell): There is no good reason to privilege one of the colours/shapes I experience the table as having over the others as the "real" property. Therefore what we perceive are sense-data, not the object's properties themselves.

The response (direct realist via relational properties): The table genuinely has the property "looking brown to a normal observer in normal light from this angle" — this is a *relational* property of the table, not a property of a separate mental sense-datum. Some properties an object has independent of minds (intrinsic); some it has in relation to being perceived (relational). Both are properties of the *object*. Furthermore, for shape, we have a privileged candidate (rectangular, not obtuse) because the table's *behaviour* (e.g. fitting through a doorway) confirms one shape over the others.

Who has the stronger position and why: The relational-property reply is conceptually clean and saves direct realism from this version of the variation argument. It cracks under stronger pressure (illusion and especially hallucination), but on perceptual variation alone, direct realism plausibly wins.

---

### [Argument: Illusion] — Standard formulation

* P1: We perceive something having some property F (e.g. a pencil that is crooked).
* P2: When we perceive something as having property F, then there is something that is F.
* P3: In an illusion, the physical object does not have property F (the pencil is not crooked).
* C1: Therefore, in illusions, what has property F is something mental — a sense-datum.
* C2: Therefore, in illusions, we see sense-data, not physical objects, immediately.
* P4: Illusions can be subjectively indistinguishable from veridical perception.
* C3: Therefore, we see the same thing — sense-data — in both illusions and veridical perception.
* C4: Therefore, in all cases, we see sense-data, not physical objects, immediately.
* C5: Therefore, direct realism is false.

**Weakest premise:** P2. The direct realist denies the "phenomenal principle" (something must literally have F whenever we perceive something as F). The pencil has the relational property "looking crooked when half-submerged" without anything being literally crooked.

**Examiner's attack question:** Why must there be something that *is* F merely because something *appears* F? Doesn't the relational-property reply dissolve P2?

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Illusion]

The objection (illusion argument): In an illusion, *something* must be the bearer of the apparent property — and since the physical object isn't, it must be a sense-datum. Generalising to indistinguishable veridical cases, we always perceive sense-data.

The response (direct realist): Reject P2. The pencil has the *relational* property "looks crooked when half-submerged in water." There is no need to posit a separate mental thing that is literally crooked. Usually things look F when they are F, but the two properties can come apart, and that's fine — it's still the *object* that has both.

Who has the stronger position and why: The reply is plausible for illusions but is exposed when we move to hallucinations, where there is no physical object to bear *any* relational property. So direct realism survives the illusion argument but must say more about hallucination.

---

### [Argument: Hallucination] — Standard formulation

* P1: In a hallucination, we perceive something having some property F.
* P2: When we perceive something as having property F, there is something that is F.
* P3: In a hallucination, we don't perceive a physical object at all.
* C1: Therefore what we perceive must be mental — a sense-datum.
* P4: Hallucinations can be subjectively indistinguishable from veridical perceptions.
* C2: Therefore we see the same thing — sense-data — in both hallucinations and veridical perception.
* C3: Therefore, in all cases, we see sense-data, not physical objects.
* C4: Therefore direct realism is false.

**Weakest premise:** P2 again — and additionally the move from "subjectively indistinguishable" (C2) to "same kind of mental state." The disjunctivist denies this.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does subjective indistinguishability (looking the same from the inside) entail being the same kind of mental state? If not, why should hallucinations dictate the structure of veridical perception?

---

### [Argument: Disjunctive Theory of Perception] — Direct realist response to hallucination

* P1: When something looks a certain way to me, one of two quite different things may be happening: either (a) I perceive a mind-independent physical object that has F, or (b) it merely seems to me that there is something that is F (hallucination).
* P2: These are *two distinct kinds of mental state*, not a common kind that varies in connection to the world.
* P3: Subjective indistinguishability does not establish identity of mental kind.
* C1: Therefore the argument from hallucination's move from "indistinguishable from veridical perception" to "same as veridical perception" fails.
* C2: Therefore hallucinations tell us nothing about the structure of genuine perception. Veridical perception is a relation to the world; hallucination is not.

**Weakest premise:** P2. Why think the two states are fundamentally different *kinds* given they appear identical to the subject? An opponent will say this is ad hoc — invoked solely to save direct realism.

**Examiner's attack question:** What positive evidence does the disjunctivist have that hallucinations and veridical perceptions are different kinds of mental state, beyond the fact that direct realism needs them to be?

---

### [Argument: Time-Lag] — Standard formulation

* P1: Light, sound, smell etc. take time to travel from physical objects to our sense organs (light from the sun: ~8 minutes; light from distant stars: years).
* P2: What we perceive is therefore the object as it *was*, not as it is now.
* C1: We could even see the sun normally for 8 minutes after it had blown up.
* C2: Therefore we don't perceive physical objects directly.

**Weakest premise:** The inference to C2. The time-lag is a fact about *how* we perceive, not a fact about whether the immediate object of perception is mental.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does the time-lag show we perceive sense-data, or only that we perceive physical objects *as they were a moment ago*?

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Time-Lag]

The objection: We don't see the sun, we see light from the sun (now) which originated 8 minutes ago. So we perceive the medium, not the object.

The response (direct realist): Confusion of "how we perceive" with "what we perceive." Light is part of the *story* of how we see the sun, not what we see *instead of* the sun. Compare: "Can you see the lake?" / "Can you see the light reflecting off the lake?" — the latter is unusual phrasing; ordinarily we just see the lake. We don't perceive light waves themselves directly except in special cases. The time-lag only shows that we literally see *into the past* (the world as it was a moment ago, or in astronomy, the distant past).

Who has the stronger position and why: Direct realism wins this exchange comfortably. The time-lag argument conflates the causal mechanism of perception with its direct object.

---

### Theory — The Openness Argument (a positive case for direct realism)

Core claim: Our perceptual experience presents the world to us *as* mind-independent objects; we describe what we see using physical-object concepts because that is what perception delivers.

What this commits the thinker to: That if we perceived sense-data instead, perception would be radically misleading about its own nature — and that strong reasons would be needed to accept this.

What this rules out: Smooth acceptance of any view (indirect realism, idealism) on which the immediate object of perception is mental.

Key terminology:
- **Openness** — perception's apparent direct-contact-with-the-world character; we describe perceptual experience necessarily using physical-object vocabulary ("a desk with pens and paper", not "coloured patches in spatial relations").

The argument's force: Try to describe a normal scene purely in sense-datum vocabulary. You can't — every shape is "plant-shaped" or "table-shaped" because we lack a vocabulary for sense-data divorced from physical-object concepts. This makes direct realism the default and shifts the burden of proof onto its rivals.

## SECTION 2 — INDIRECT REALISM

### Theory — Indirect Realism

Core claim: We perceive mind-independent physical objects via, or in virtue of, perceiving mind-dependent sense-data that are caused by and represent those physical objects.

What this commits the thinker to: That sense-data exist as mental items; that there is a causal relation between physical objects and sense-data; that sense-data represent (more or less accurately) the physical objects causing them.

What this rules out: Direct realism (we never directly perceive physical objects); idealism (physical objects are real and mind-independent).

Key terminology:
- **Sense-datum** — the mental, mind-dependent content of a perceptual experience; private; appearance-only; exists only while experienced.
- **Representation** — the relation by which sense-data convey information about (depict, stand in for) physical objects, traditionally explained via *resemblance*, more recently via *causal correlation*.

---

### [Argument: General Case for Indirect Realism] — Combining the anti-direct-realism arguments

* P1: There are many perceptual experiences in which what we experience is not the way the physical object is (perceptual variation, illusion, hallucination).
* P2: When we perceive something as having property F, there is something that is F.
* C1: In such cases, what we perceive are sense-data (mental items that bear the experienced properties).
* P3: Such cases are subjectively indistinguishable from veridical perception.
* P4: When two perceptual experiences are subjectively indistinguishable, they are perceptual experiences of the same *kind of thing* (best hypothesis given P3).
* C2: Therefore we always perceive sense-data — even in veridical perception.
* P5: In veridical perception (and illusion), the sense-data are caused by and represent physical objects.
* C3: Therefore we perceive physical objects indirectly, via sense-data.

**Weakest premise:** P4 (the "common kind" assumption) — directly attacked by disjunctivism. Also P2 (the phenomenal principle).

**Examiner's attack question:** Why accept P4 against the disjunctivist? Subjective indistinguishability does not entail sameness of mental kind.

---

### Distinction — Locke's Primary vs Secondary Qualities (Essay, Bk 2, Ch. 8)

**Primary qualities:** Properties that are "utterly inseparable" from a physical object — the object has them in and of itself, regardless of being perceived. Locke's list: extension (size), shape, motion, number, solidity.

**Secondary qualities:** Properties that are "nothing but powers" in the object to produce sensations in us — colour, sound, taste, smell, temperature.

**The key difference:** Primary qualities are *intrinsic* (mind-independent and present whether perceived or not); secondary qualities are *dispositional/relational* (defined by their power to produce sensations in perceivers).

**Common confusion to avoid:** "Solidity" doesn't mean "non-liquid/non-gas" — it means *taking up space and excluding other physical objects from that space*. Liquids and gases also have solidity in this sense.

Locke's central claim about resemblance:
- Our perceptual experience of *primary* qualities resembles the qualities as they are in the object.
- Our perceptual experience of *secondary* qualities does *not* resemble anything in the object — secondary qualities arise from the primary qualities of the object's "imperceptible parts" (its atomic/molecular structure).

This grounds indirect realism: the world as we experience it (rich with colour, smell, taste, warmth) is quite different from the world as it is in itself (particles in motion in space). Therefore we don't perceive physical objects directly; we perceive sense-data that *partially* resemble them (in primary qualities) and *fail to resemble* them (in secondary qualities).

---

## SECTION 3 — INDIRECT REALISM'S TWO BIG PROBLEMS

Indirect realism faces two distinct sceptical worries: (i) can we know physical objects *exist* at all? (ii) can we know what they are *like*?

### Issue 1 — Scepticism about the *Existence* of Mind-Independent Objects

[Argument: Indirect Realism Leads to Scepticism About External Existence] — Russell (Problems of Philosophy, Ch. 2)

* P1: Indirect realism says we perceive only sense-data directly.
* P2: To know that physical objects cause sense-data, we must first know that physical objects exist.
* P3: But the only access we have to physical objects is via sense-data.
* C1: Therefore we cannot know that physical objects exist.
* C2: Therefore (since indirect realism itself claims they exist) indirect realism cannot be known to be true even if true.

**Weakest premise:** P2 read strictly, perhaps. The indirect realist may argue that we can *infer* the best explanation rather than needing antecedent knowledge.

**Examiner's attack question:** If all we directly access is sense-data, can the existence of physical objects ever be more than a hypothesis?

---

### Russell's Response 1 (Rejected) — The Argument from Other People

* P1: Sense-data are private, so no two people share numerically the same sense-datum.
* P2: People at the same place and time have very similar sense-data.
* P3: The best explanation is that there are physical objects causing their sense-data.
* C: Physical objects exist.

**Why Russell rejects this:** It assumes that other people exist. But people are physical objects — so this begs the question. We were trying to establish that physical objects exist; we cannot start by assuming the existence of certain ones (other people).

---

### [Argument: Russell's Best-Hypothesis Response] — Russell (accepted)

* P1: Either physical objects exist and cause my sense-data, or they don't.
* P2: I cannot prove either claim is true or false (deductively).
* C1: Therefore I must treat them as competing hypotheses.
* P3: The hypothesis that physical objects exist and cause my sense-data is the *better* hypothesis, because it explains the regularities and continuities in my experience (e.g. the cat I saw in the corner is later on the sofa — this makes no sense if the cat doesn't exist when unperceived; the existence of the cat as a continuous physical object explains my disjointed perceptions).
* C2: Therefore physical objects exist and cause my sense-data.

**Weakest premise:** P3 — Berkeley will exploit this by arguing that *idealism* (with God as the cause of regularities) is just as good or better a hypothesis. The argument is also non-deductive (inference to best explanation), so cannot deliver certainty — a problem for any realist who wanted to defeat scepticism.

**Examiner's attack question:** Is "physical objects cause my sense-data" really the *best* explanation, or just a familiar one? Why prefer it over the brain-in-a-vat or Berkeleyan-idealist alternatives that explain the same data?

---

### [Argument: Locke's Argument from the Involuntary Nature of Experience] — Locke (Essay, Bk 4, Ch. 11)

* P1: In perception, I cannot avoid having certain sense-data produced in my mind.
* P2: When I turn to memory or imagination, I can choose what I experience.
* P3: Perceptual experiences are therefore not produced by my mind alone.
* C: Therefore they must be produced by some exterior cause — namely, physical objects.

**Weakest premise:** The inference to "physical objects" specifically. Involuntariness shows the cause is not me — but Berkeley will exploit this by arguing the cause is *another mind* (God), not a physical object.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does involuntariness entail an external *physical* cause, or only an external *cause* (which could equally be another mind)?

---

### [Argument: Argument from Coherence Between Senses] — Locke + Trotter Cockburn

* P1: One and the same physical object causes perceptual experiences across different sensory modalities (I see a fire and feel its heat; I see a dog and hear its bark).
* P2: These different sensory experiences confirm and predict each other in systematic ways (we can tell from sight what something will sound or feel like).
* P3: If we change one experience by a physical action (e.g. moving away from a sound source), the other experiences change in correlated ways (the sound becomes quieter).
* P4: The best explanation of this systematic cross-modal coherence is that there is a single mind-independent object causing all the modalities.
* C: Therefore mind-independent physical objects exist.

Locke's extended example: When I write on a piece of paper, sight and proprioception (sense of hand-movement) connect; what I see is constrained by what I do; once written, the words are no longer under my voluntary control; if read aloud by someone else, what I hear matches what I wrote. This systematic interaction "leaves little reason for doubt" that the words exist mind-independently.

**Weakest premise:** Same as Locke's previous argument — the inference to *physical* objects rather than another mind. Berkeley will say God's coordination of our experiences explains the same data.

**Examiner's attack question:** Is the best explanation of cross-modal coherence really a *physical* object, or could it be a coordinating *mind* (Berkeley's God)?

---

### Theory — "Existence Is Not a Hypothesis" Reinterpretation of Indirect Realism

Core claim: Sense-data are *how* we perceive physical objects, not *what we perceive instead of* physical objects. They mediate access; they don't block it. The existence of the external world is therefore not a hypothesis but is *experienced* in perception.

What this commits the thinker to: A reading of "indirect" as "via" rather than "by inference from." Sense-data are part of the structure of perception, not interposed obstacles between us and the world.

What this rules out: The Russell/Locke/Trotter Cockburn picture on which sense-data "come between" us and the world and physical objects must be inferred as their best-explanation cause.

Key terminology:
- **Mediation (vs interposition)** — sense-data structure perception without blocking it.

This is a significant move because it concedes the openness intuition while keeping the indirect-realist commitment to mind-dependent perceptual content. It dissolves Russell's scepticism worry — but at the cost of looking suspiciously close to direct realism.

---

### Issue 2 — Scepticism about the *Nature* of Mind-Independent Objects

This is a separate worry: even *granting* that physical objects exist, can we know what they are like?

The indirect realist owes us an account of how sense-data *represent* physical objects. Locke says they *resemble* them (in primary qualities at least). Berkeley attacks this.

---

### [Argument: Berkeley's Argument Against Resemblance] — Berkeley (Three Dialogues, First Dialogue, p. 25)

The argument has two distinct strands:

**Strand 1: The Variability Argument**
* P1: Sense-data are perpetually fleeting and variable (a circle viewed from one angle yields circular sense-data; from another, oval sense-data).
* P2: A physical object is fixed and constant (the plate has just one shape).
* C1: Something constantly changing cannot resemble something fixed and constant.
* C2: Furthermore, no one experience is a privileged "true copy" of the object — we cannot non-arbitrarily say *which* sense-datum truly resembles the object.
* C3: Therefore sense-data cannot resemble physical objects.

**Strand 2: The Like-Only-Like Argument**
* P1: We do not directly experience physical objects (only sense-data).
* P2: We can only have ideas of qualities (size, shape, etc.) from our perceptual experience.
* P3: Therefore the only ideas we have of qualities are ideas of *experienced* qualities (mind-dependent ones).
* P4: We cannot meaningfully say that what we do not experience (mind-independent qualities) resembles what we do experience (mind-dependent qualities) — there is nothing like a perceptual experience except another perceptual experience.
* C: Therefore we cannot meaningfully say that sense-data resemble physical objects.

**Weakest premise:** P4 in Strand 2 — the claim that resemblance only holds within a single ontological kind is itself controversial. Why can't a mental and a physical thing share a structural property like shape?

**Examiner's attack question:** Why must "resemblance" require shared ontological kind? Can't a mental representation share a structural feature (e.g. squareness) with a physical thing without being the same kind of thing?

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Resemblance vs Causal Representation]

The objection (Berkeley): Locke's resemblance claim is incoherent. Sense-data cannot resemble physical objects (variability + like-only-like). Therefore we cannot know the nature of physical objects from sense-data — and indirect realism collapses into scepticism about the nature of the external world.

The response (modern indirect realist): Concede resemblance is problematic. Replace it with *systematic causal correlation*. Sense-data represent physical objects by being caused by them in detailed, regular, structure-preserving ways. Representation doesn't require resemblance.

Who has the stronger position and why: Berkeley scores against the *resemblance* version of indirect realism (Locke's). The causal-correlation version sidesteps the like-only-like objection — but at a real cost: causes can be very unlike their effects (smoke is unlike fire), so even a tight causal correlation may not tell us what physical objects are *like in themselves*. The indirect realist still has a residual nature-scepticism worry.

## SECTION 4 — BERKELEY'S IDEALISM

### Theory — Berkeley's Idealism

Core claim: Reality is dependent on minds. Only minds (which perceive) and ideas (what minds perceive) exist. Physical objects are bundles of ideas — they exist only insofar as they are perceived (or perceived by God).

What this commits the thinker to: *Esse est percipi (aut percipere)* — to be is to be perceived (or to perceive). No mind-independent matter. Some mind (God) must perceive things continuously to keep them in existence.

What this rules out: Both forms of realism — there are no mind-independent physical objects.

Key terminology:
- **Idea** — for Berkeley, the content of a perception or thought; mind-dependent.
- **Spirit / mind** — the *active* cause of ideas; perceives, thinks, wills. Ideas are *passive*; minds are active.
- **Esse est percipi** — to be is to be perceived. The slogan of idealism.

---

### Berkeley's First Move — Secondary Qualities are Mind-Dependent

[Argument: Pleasure/Pain Argument for Mind-Dependence of Secondary Qualities] — Berkeley (First Dialogue, pp. 1–12)

* P1: Intense heat, cold, light, sound, pressure are experienced as pain.
* P2: Pain is obviously mind-dependent (nothing in a physical object is "in pain").
* P3: We feel one unified sensation of *painful heat* (light, etc.) — heat is not a separate quality merely *causing* pain; the heat-sensation and the pain are inseparable.
* P4: Generally, our perceptions of secondary qualities are accompanied by some form of pleasure or pain.
* C: Therefore secondary qualities are mind-dependent.

**Weakest premise:** P3 — the claim that we cannot distinguish heat-sensation from the pain it causes. Empirically, we plausibly can (we distinguish sweetness from the pleasure associated with it). If we can, the hot/cold quality might exist in the object even if the pain is in us.

**Examiner's attack question:** Is Berkeley's empirical claim about the inseparability of heat-sensation and pain-sensation actually true? Doesn't moderate heat give a sensation of warmth without pain?

---

[Argument: Perceptual Variation Argument for Mind-Dependence of Secondary Qualities] — Berkeley

* P1: A cloud from a distance looks pink; up close, it loses its colour.
* P2: A solid object viewed through a microscope appears to have different colours than when viewed normally.
* P3: Different animals perceive the colours of objects differently.
* P4: Different lighting conditions (daylight, candlelight) change the apparent colour without changing the object.
* C: Therefore colours are appearances, not properties of physical objects. (Generalising: secondary qualities are mind-dependent.)

Berkeley's reply to the "secondary qualities really exist as physical particles in motion" rebuttal: If sound is just vibration of the air, we can't hear sound (vibrations are perceived by sight or touch). If colour is just photons of certain energies, we can't see colour (we don't see photons). So redefining secondary qualities scientifically eliminates them from perception — a counter-intuitive result.

**Weakest premise:** P4 — different lighting can be characterised as a different relation between object and perceiver, allowing the colour to be a relational property of the object rather than fully mind-dependent.

**Examiner's attack question:** Can the indirect realist accept Berkeley's argument here while resisting his next step (extending the argument to primary qualities)?

---

### Berkeley's Second Move — The Primary/Secondary Distinction Collapses

[Argument: Variation Applies Equally to Primary Qualities] — Berkeley (First Dialogue, pp. 13–15)

* P1: What looks small to me may look huge to a small animal.
* P2: What looks small from a distance looks large up close.
* P3: What looks smooth to the naked eye appears craggy under a microscope.
* P4: A circle looks circular straight on but elliptical from an angle.
* P5: Motion isn't constant — to a creature thinking faster than us (a housefly), our fastest movements appear leisurely.
* P6 (parity premise from secondary qualities): When an object appears to have many colours depending on perception, we can't say it has one *real* colour mind-independently.
* C1: By parity, we can't say a physical object has one real shape, size, motion, independent of how it is perceived.
* C2: Therefore primary qualities are just as mind-dependent as secondary qualities.

**Weakest premise:** The parity reasoning. The indirect realist can argue that primary-quality variation is *systematically explicable* in terms of viewing-conditions while secondary-quality variation is not — so primary qualities can still be assigned to the object via the relations.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does perceptual variation in primary qualities really show they are mind-dependent — or only that their *appearance* is relationally determined while the underlying property remains intrinsic?

---

### [Argument: All Objects of Perception are Mind-Dependent] — Berkeley

* P1: Everything we perceive is either a primary or a secondary quality.
* P2: Both primary and secondary qualities are mind-dependent.
* C: Therefore nothing we perceive exists independently of the mind — the objects of perception are entirely mind-dependent.

**Weakest premise:** P1. Direct realism rejects this — its "openness" claim is precisely that we perceive *physical objects*, not just bundles of qualities. If we perceive the object itself, then even if some of its perceived qualities are mind-dependent, the object isn't.

**Examiner's attack question:** What is the strongest case for Berkeley's P1? Why couldn't perception present us with the *substance* (the physical object as a whole) rather than just a bundle of qualities?

---

### Berkeley's Three Arguments Against Mind-Independent Physical Objects (First Dialogue, pp. 19–23)

**Argument 1: The "Material Substratum" is Incoherent**

[Argument: Material Substratum] — Berkeley

* P1: Suppose we perceive only primary and secondary qualities (established above).
* P2: For there to be mind-independent physical objects, there must be something that *bears* primary and secondary qualities and unifies them into one thing — a "material substratum."
* P3: This substratum is, by definition, distinct from the qualities it bears.
* P4: We perceive only primary and secondary qualities — never the substratum itself.
* P5: If substance exists unperceived, it exists without any qualities (since all qualities are perceived qualities).
* C1: The concept of material substratum is therefore incoherent — it is the concept of something we can say nothing about, that has no properties.
* C2: Therefore mind-independent physical objects cannot exist.

Locke's concession (used against him): Locke himself accepted that the idea of substance was the idea of "something I know not what" — confirming Berkeley's diagnosis.

**Weakest premise:** P5. Realists can argue substance has properties via its primary qualities — extension, shape, etc. — without needing the perceived experience of those properties to be the substance itself.

**Examiner's attack question:** Must substance be totally distinct from its qualities, or can a physical object simply *be* the bearer-with-its-properties without the bearer being a separate, characterless thing?

---

**Argument 2: Experience Supports Idealism, Not Realism**

[Argument: Experience-Supports-Idealism] — Berkeley

* P1: All we perceive are primary and secondary qualities, not mind-independent physical objects.
* C1: Therefore experience cannot verify the hypothesis of a mind-independent physical world.
* P2: The hypothesis of physical substance is not even *suggested* by experience.
* C2: Close attention to experience supports the claim that all there is (or all we can say there is) is what we can experience.
* P3: What we experience are ideas.
* C3: Therefore experience supports idealism, not realism.

**Weakest premise:** The move from "experience does not verify physical substance" to "experience supports idealism." Lack of evidence for X is not evidence against X.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does the absence of direct experiential support for mind-independent matter really *favour* idealism over agnosticism?

---

**Argument 3: Realism Leads to Scepticism**

[Argument: Realism Implies Scepticism] — Berkeley

* P1: If physical objects are mind-independent, they are not directly perceived.
* P2: We cannot connect our experiences to something "beyond" them that we can't even describe.
* P3: We cannot know that ideas accurately represent something existing fully independently of them.
* C1: If there are mind-independent physical objects, we can know nothing about them.
* P4: But if there is no mind-independent reality, then what we perceive *is* what there is — namely ideas — and so perception gives us knowledge of what there is.
* C2: Therefore idealism, not realism, secures knowledge from perception.

**Weakest premise:** P4 doesn't actually support idealism on positive grounds — it just shows idealism would be more knowledge-friendly *if true*. That's not an argument that it *is* true.

**Examiner's attack question:** Is Berkeley arguing for idealism, or merely *against* realism's scepticism? Does the impossibility-of-knowing-mind-independent-things really entail there are no mind-independent things?

---

### [Argument: Berkeley's "Master Argument"] — Berkeley (First Dialogue, p. 21)

* P1: If I can conceive of any mixture of qualities (any sensible object) existing outside the mind, then it can.
* P2: Try to conceive of a tree existing unperceived by anyone.
* P3: But in conceiving of it, I am thinking of it — so the tree is being conceived by my mind.
* C1: I cannot conceive of a tree that is *not* conceived by any mind; I can only conceive of a tree-as-conceived.
* C2: Therefore, mind-independent existence is inconceivable.
* C3: Therefore no mind-independent objects exist.

**Weakest premise:** The slide from "I cannot conceive of a tree without my conceiving" to "I cannot conceive of a tree as not being conceived." Berkeley confuses the *act of thinking* with *what the thought is about*.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does the master argument confuse a thought with what it's about? Can't I think *of a tree existing unperceived* even though my *thinking* of it is itself a mental act?

---

### [Topic of Dispute: The Master Argument]

The objection (Berkeley): To conceive of a tree existing unperceived is impossible because the conceiving makes it perceived. Therefore mind-independent existence is inconceivable.

The response (standard textbook critique): Distinguish:
* P1: Thoughts cannot exist outside the mind (thoughts are mental events).
* C1: My thinking of a tree is mind-dependent.
* P2: But what a thought is *about* is not the same as the thought itself.
* C2: From "my thinking of a tree is mind-dependent" it does *not* follow that *what I am thinking of* (the tree itself) is mind-dependent.

The master argument is a use-mention confusion: it conflates the *act* of representing (which is necessarily mental) with the *object* of representation (which need not be).

Who has the stronger position and why: The critique decisively wins. Berkeley's master argument is generally regarded as a fallacy. He may have other arguments for idealism, but this one fails.

---

### [Argument: Idealism and God] — Berkeley (Third Dialogue, pp. 41–2)

The need for God: If physical objects are bundles of ideas, what causes our perceptions?

* P1: Physical objects (= bundles of ideas) are mind-dependent. Possible causes of my perceptions: ideas, my mind, or another mind.
* P2: Ideas don't cause anything (ideas are passive; only minds are active).
* P3: If physical objects depended on my mind, I could control what I perceive.
* P4: But I can't — perception is involuntary, unlike imagination.
* C1: Therefore physical objects don't depend solely on my mind.
* C2: Therefore they must exist in another mind, which wills that I perceive them.
* P5: The complexity, regularity, and systematicity of my perceptions indicate this mind is wise, powerful, good — beyond what I can comprehend.
* C3: Therefore that mind is God.

**Weakest premise:** P2 — Berkeley's claim that ideas are passive and only minds active is a substantive metaphysical claim, not obviously true. Modern objectors might appeal to causal explanations not grounded in mental agency.

**Examiner's attack question:** What grounds Berkeley's claim that "ideas are passive, only minds are active"? Is this empirical or stipulative?

---

### Why Ideas Cannot Be Causes (Berkeley, pp. 32ff.)

Berkeley's reasoning: Should we say thoughts think themselves, or the mind thinks them? Should we say perceptions cause our experience of them, or the mind perceives? The latter is clearly more natural. Ideas are passive — we *do* things with ideas (think, will, perceive); ideas don't *do* anything to us. Only the mind acts.

This is the basis for two further Berkeleyan arguments:

**Matter-Cannot-Be-The-Cause Argument 1:**
* P1: Matter (in the normal sense — mind-independent and possessing primary qualities) cannot exist (from the previous arguments).
* C1: Therefore to say anything sensible about "matter" we must mean our perceptions of it.
* P2: What we perceive (primary and secondary qualities) are ideas.
* P3: Ideas are passive.
* C2: Therefore whatever causes our perceptions must be a *mind*, not matter.

**Matter-Cannot-Be-The-Cause Argument 2 (the interaction problem):**
* P1: Realism requires that mind-independent physical objects causally affect our minds (via sense organs and brain).
* P2: We have no understanding of how something physical can produce a *mental* idea.
* P3: This problem (the mind-body problem) has resisted solution for centuries.
* C: Therefore the realist hypothesis faces an unresolved causal mystery, while idealism (mind causing mind-events) is more intelligible.

## SECTION 5 — ISSUES WITH BERKELEY'S IDEALISM

### Issue 1 — Problems with the Role of God

[Topic of Dispute: How Can What I Perceive Exist in God's Mind?]

The objection (against Berkeley):
* P1: My perceptions and sensations are part of *my* mind, not God's.
* P2: God can't have the perceptual experiences I have — God doesn't undergo sensations like pain.
* P3: The objects of my perception change and go out of existence, but God's mind is unchanging and eternal.
* C: Therefore what I perceive cannot be part of God's mind.

The response (Berkeley):
1. What I perceive is a *copy* of the idea in God's mind (so the originals stay in God; copies are in me).
2. The ideas of physical objects exist in God's mind not as *perceptions* but as part of God's *understanding*. God doesn't perceive red or feel pain, but knows what it is for us to undergo these experiences.
3. What I perceive (which changes) is what God *wills* me to perceive at a given time. The whole of creation exists eternally in God's understanding; things "begin to exist" when God decrees they become perceptible to creatures.

Who has the stronger position and why: Berkeley's response is internally consistent but strains common usage badly. The distinction between "ideas in God's understanding" and "ideas in my perception" preserves the theory at the cost of multiplying entities and stretching theological language. A serious worry remains: if God's "ideas" don't function like our perceptions, in what sense is the world "ideas" at all?

---

### Issue 2 — Illusions and Hallucinations

[Topic of Dispute: How Does Idealism Handle Illusions?]

The objection (Hylas/textbook): Since we perceive ideas, there must be an idea corresponding to the illusion. But we don't want to say the physical object is as it looks in the illusion (the half-submerged pencil isn't really crooked). Yet if the pencil just *is* the ideas we perceive, then the pencil *is* crooked when half-submerged. This is plainly false.

The response (Berkeley):
- We *aren't* misperceiving — what we perceive in the case of the half-submerged pencil is genuinely crooked-as-perceived.
- The illusion is only misleading if we draw false *inferences* about other ideas we'd associate with this perception (e.g. inferring it would feel crooked if touched, or look crooked when pulled out).
- To respect ordinary language, we shouldn't say "the pencil is crooked" but "the pencil *looks* crooked" — because ordinary usage of "is crooked" implies "would look crooked under normal conditions," which is false here.

Who has the stronger position and why: Berkeley's response is workable but exposes a fragility — it relies on a delicate distinction between "what we perceive" (the crooked appearance, which is reality) and "what we'd infer" (which can be wrong). It saves idealism by relocating error from perception to inference. The direct realist's relational-property reply ("the pencil has the property of looking crooked when half-submerged") arguably handles the same data more elegantly.

---

[Topic of Dispute: How Does Idealism Distinguish Hallucinations from Perceptions?]

The objection: If perception and hallucination are both just ideas in the mind, what makes one veridical and the other not?

The response (Berkeley): Two criteria distinguish hallucinations:
1. They are "dim, irregular, and confused" compared to perceptions.
2. Even if they were as vivid as perceptions, they are not coherently connected with the rest of our perceptual experience.

The deeper account: ideas in perception originate in God; ideas in hallucination don't. The two criteria are how we *tell* the difference; the metaphysical difference is in their origin.

Who has the stronger position and why: The objection that vividness/coherence are differences of *degree* (whereas perception/hallucination differ in *kind*) cuts deep against Berkeley's surface criteria. He partially patches this by appealing to divine origin, but this is a metaphysical fix not available to the perceiver from the inside.

---

### Issue 3 — Solipsism

[Topic of Dispute: Does Idealism Collapse into Solipsism?]

The objection: Berkeley's three arguments against mind-independent objects, starting from "everything I perceive is mind-dependent," seem to lead to the conclusion that all that exists is *my* experience. If all I perceive are ideas, what reason do I have to think other minds exist? After all, I never perceive minds (including God).

The response (Berkeley):
* P1: The mind is what (actively) perceives, thinks, wills, while ideas are passive.
* P2: I am aware of myself as capable of this activity.
* C1: Therefore I am not my ideas, but a mind.
* P3: Being a mind myself, I have a *notion* of what a mind is (even though I have no *idea* of a mind in the strict sense).
* C2: Therefore other minds are at least possible (intelligible).
* P4: My perceptions don't originate in my mind (perception is involuntary).
* C3: Therefore they are caused by some other mind.
* C4: The complexity and regularity of my experience indicates this mind is God.

For other finite minds (other people): Berkeley admits their existence is a *matter of inference*, supported by features of my experience — much like Russell's best-hypothesis argument for physical objects.

Who has the stronger position and why: Berkeley escapes solipsism only by leaning hard on the same kind of inference-to-best-explanation that Russell uses for physical objects — and that he criticised realism for needing. There is a tu quoque worry: if inference-to-best-explanation is not enough for realism about physical objects, why is it enough for realism about other minds?

## SECTION 6 — LOCKE vs BERKELEY ON THE PRIMARY/SECONDARY QUALITY DISTINCTION

### Locke vs Berkeley — The Primary/Secondary Distinction

**Locke's position:** Primary qualities (extension, shape, motion, number, solidity) are intrinsic mind-independent properties of physical objects. Our perceptions of them resemble the qualities as they actually are. Secondary qualities (colour, sound, taste, smell, temperature) are dispositional powers in the object to produce sensations in us; our perceptions of them do not resemble anything in the object.

**Berkeley's position:** No such distinction holds. Whatever argument shows secondary qualities are mind-dependent (perceptual variation, the pleasure/pain inseparability) shows the same for primary qualities. Both are equally mind-dependent. Therefore both are ideas — and physical objects (= bundles of these qualities) are mind-dependent collections of ideas.

**The crux of the disagreement:** Whether perceptual variation has the same force against primary qualities as against secondary. Locke says no — primary qualities have a privileged status (they are intrinsic, science describes only them). Berkeley says yes — variation cuts equally both ways, and there is no principled basis for the asymmetry.

**Who has the stronger argument and why:** Berkeley's parity argument is forceful and has been historically influential. But Locke (and modern indirect realists) can resist it by pointing out that primary-quality variation is *systematically explicable* by viewing-conditions — the rectangular table looks obtuse from an angle by a calculable geometrical projection. Secondary-quality variation is not explicable in this systematic way (the cold-and-hot-water case for warmth, the perceiver-dependence of colour). So primary qualities preserve their claim to be intrinsic. Verdict: Berkeley's argument lands hits but doesn't decisively defeat Locke; the modern consensus tends towards a refined distinction (primary = scientifically tractable intrinsic properties; secondary = perceiver-relative).

## SECTION 7 — DIRECT REALISM vs INDIRECT REALISM vs IDEALISM (the three-way contest)

### Direct Realism vs Indirect Realism — On the Immediate Object of Perception

**Direct realism's position:** The immediate object of perception is the mind-independent physical object itself; perception is openness to the world. Where appearances diverge from reality (variation, illusion), this is explained by the object having relational properties ("looking F from here"). Hallucinations are a different kind of state altogether (disjunctivism).

**Indirect realism's position:** The immediate object of perception is a mind-dependent sense-datum, caused by and representing the physical object. The phenomenal principle (when we perceive something as F, something is F) plus the indistinguishability of veridical/non-veridical experiences (common-kind assumption) drive the conclusion.

**The crux of the disagreement:** Whether indistinguishable veridical and non-veridical experiences must be experiences of the same kind of thing (indirect realism) or can be radically different states (disjunctivist direct realism); and whether the phenomenal principle (P2 of the illusion argument) holds.

**Who has the stronger argument and why:** Direct realism preserves the openness intuition and is methodologically conservative. Indirect realism handles illusion/hallucination more naturally (everyone perceives "something" — sense-data — even when no object is there). Disjunctivism's claim that hallucination and perception are different mental kinds despite indistinguishability looks ad hoc unless independent grounds are given. Net: indirect realism has a slight edge on uniformity of explanation, direct realism on intuitive fit with experience.

### Indirect Realism vs Idealism — On the Existence of Mind-Independent Matter

**Indirect realism's position:** Sense-data are caused by mind-independent physical objects; the existence of such objects is the best explanation of our experience.

**Idealism's position:** "Mind-independent matter" is incoherent (the substratum problem) and unnecessary — God's willing of ideas in our minds explains the regularities just as well, without the unintelligible mind-matter causal interaction.

**The crux of the disagreement:** Whether "matter" or "God-willed ideas" is the better explanation of the involuntariness, coherence, and regularity of our perceptual experience.

**Who has the stronger argument and why:** Idealism gains on intelligibility (no mind-body interaction puzzle) but loses on theoretical economy (we need God) and on common sense (matter exists; reality persists when unperceived). Most contemporary philosophers reject idealism, but Berkeley's challenge to coherently characterise mind-independent substance remains a live problem.
