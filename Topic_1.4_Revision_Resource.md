## SECTION 0 — FRAMING DISTINCTIONS

### Distinction — Philosophical Scepticism vs Normal Incredulity

**Philosophical scepticism:** A general challenge to our usual *justifications* for knowledge claims, typically generated by hyperbolic doubt (e.g. brain-in-a-vat scenarios). It is non-contextual — it doubts our justifications regardless of any specific everyday reason to doubt.

**Normal incredulity:** Ordinary, context-bound doubt — having specific, situational reasons to question a particular claim (e.g. doubting I have two hands after an accident in which I can't feel my left arm).

**The key difference (one-sentence exam answer):** Normal incredulity arises *from* a particular reason to doubt within a context; philosophical scepticism is general and proceeds without any everyday reason to doubt — it challenges our justifications themselves.

**Common confusion to avoid:** Students often dismiss philosophical scepticism as "unreasonable" because it has no practical consequences. But this misunderstands its function — scepticism is not a *belief* one acts on; it is a *challenge* designed to expose what justifies our claims to know. Its value is methodological, not behavioural.

Three further features distinguishing philosophical scepticism from normal incredulity:
1. It is uninterested in everyday reasons to doubt; it generates doubts from imagined extreme scenarios.
2. It has no practical consequences (a sceptic still walks around tables, not through them).
3. It invents very unlikely situations (brain in a vat, evil demon) yet insists they are *relevant* — we should be able to rule them out if we are to claim knowledge.

---

### Distinction — Local vs Global Scepticism

**Local scepticism:** Scepticism about a *specific* claim, or more commonly about a particular *area* or *branch* of supposed knowledge (e.g. doubt about religious knowledge while accepting scientific knowledge; doubt about astronomy without doubting biology).

**Global scepticism:** Scepticism extending without limit — calling all knowledge into question, especially knowledge of an external mind-independent world.

**The key difference:** Local scepticism is bounded; global scepticism is unbounded. Normal incredulity is *always* local; philosophical scepticism *can be* either local or global, but the most challenging version is global.

**Common confusion to avoid:** Don't conflate "global scepticism" with "the claim that nothing is known." Global scepticism *challenges* whether we can know — it does not assert we cannot. (The bald claim "nothing is known" is self-defeating: we'd then know that nothing is known.)

---

### Distinction — Hyperbolic Doubt vs Reasonable Doubt

**Hyperbolic doubt:** Methodological doubt that demands we rule out even very unlikely possibilities (e.g. evil demons, brain-in-vat scenarios) before counting something as known.

**Reasonable doubt:** Context-sensitive doubt limited to genuine, practically relevant possibilities of error.

**The key difference:** Hyperbolic doubt is methodologically maximal — it admits any logically possible defeater; reasonable doubt is calibrated to the situation and what is at stake.

**Common confusion to avoid:** Hyperbolic doubt is not the same as *believing* the sceptical scenarios are true or even probable. It is a tool for testing the foundations of belief, not a positive claim about the world.

## SECTION 1 — WHAT PHILOSOPHICAL SCEPTICISM IS

### Theory — Philosophical Scepticism

Core claim: Our usual justifications for claiming our beliefs amount to knowledge are inadequate; we cannot rule out alternative possibilities (e.g. being a brain in a vat) which, if true, would mean our beliefs are false or unjustified.

What this commits the thinker to: That for me to know that p, I must have justification adequate to rule out the relevant sceptical alternatives to p; that meeting this standard is hard or impossible.

What this rules out: The naïve view that everyday justifications (seeing, feeling, remembering) are unproblematically sufficient for knowledge. Also rules out the bare claim "we know nothing" (self-defeating).

Key terminology:
- **Justification** — the epistemic backing that turns true belief into knowledge (on the tripartite view).
- **Hyperbolic doubt** — Descartes' method: doubt anything that *could* be doubted, however unlikely.
- **Sceptical hypothesis** — a possibility (brain in a vat, evil demon, dreaming) which, if true, would mean my beliefs are radically mistaken.
- **Subjective indistinguishability** — two experiences (e.g. veridical perception and being-deceived) being identical from the inside.

---

### The Role/Function of Scepticism within Epistemology

Theory — Scepticism as a Challenge (not a doctrine)

Core claim: Scepticism is best understood not as the doctrine "nothing is known" but as a methodological challenge: "Rule out these sceptical possibilities — and tell us how."

What this commits the thinker to: Treating sceptical scenarios as *tools* for clarifying our concepts of knowledge and justification, not as serious empirical hypotheses.

What this rules out: The strawman version of scepticism as a self-refuting nihilism about all knowledge.

Key terminology:
- **Scepticism-as-theory** — the (rejected) reading on which the sceptic positively asserts that we know nothing.
- **Scepticism-as-challenge** — the preferred reading: the sceptic asks us to rule out alternatives and demonstrate adequate justification.

The pedagogical function: by engaging with the sceptic's challenge, we can discover *what* we know and *how* we know it. The doubts are diagnostic, not destructive.

## SECTION 2 — THE BRAIN IN A VAT

### [Argument: Brain in a Vat] — modern formulation of the sceptical challenge

* P1: It is logically possible that I am a brain in a vat, fed precisely the experiences I am having now by a supercomputer.
* P2: If I were a brain in a vat, my experiences would be qualitatively indistinguishable from the experiences I am having if I am not.
* P3: Therefore I have no evidence that distinguishes "I am not a brain in a vat" from "I am a brain in a vat."
* P4: If I cannot distinguish these possibilities, my belief that I am not a brain in a vat lacks justification.
* P5: Even if my belief that the world is roughly as I think it is happens to be true, it lacks justification.
* C1: Therefore I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat.
* C2: Therefore I do not know that my ordinary beliefs about the world (e.g. "I have two hands") are true.

**Weakest premise:** P4 — the claim that lacking the ability to discriminate sceptical alternatives entails lacking justification. Reliabilists, externalists, and contextualists all attack this premise from different angles.

**Examiner's attack question:** Does the inability to *prove* I am not a brain in a vat really show I have no *justification* for thinking I'm not? Or does it only show I lack a special kind of certainty that knowledge may not require?

---

### Three Iterations of the Brain-in-a-Vat (escalation)

The thought experiment can be ratcheted up in three stages, each broadening the scope of doubt:

1. **Sense experiences:** The supercomputer feeds my senses. Therefore I cannot know that physical objects exist or that I have a body.
2. **Memories:** The supercomputer also fabricates memories. Therefore I cannot trust memory; I cannot know anything about the past, including whether the past happened at all (perhaps I came into existence five minutes ago).
3. **Thoughts/Reasoning:** The supercomputer feeds even my thoughts. Every time I think "2 + 2 = ?" the computer makes me think "4" when the answer is in fact 5. Therefore I cannot trust my reasoning. So I cannot even know that 2 + 2 = 4.

This third stage is what makes the scenario *globally* sceptical — it threatens not just empirical knowledge but a priori knowledge as well.

**Weakest premise:** The third escalation. Even granting that perception and memory could be deceived, it is less clear that the *contents* of mathematical/logical reasoning could be replaced by contradictions without the deception being internally detectable.

**Examiner's attack question:** Can the brain-in-a-vat scenario coherently extend to mathematical reasoning, or does the sceptical scenario presuppose the very logical principles it is meant to undermine?

## SECTION 3 — DESCARTES' THREE WAVES OF DOUBT

### Wave 1 — [Argument: from Illusion] — Descartes (Meditation I, p. 1)

* P1: I have sometimes been deceived by my senses (things look small at a distance; pencils look crooked half-submerged in water).
* P2: A justification that has deceived me before is not certain.
* P3: I should not trust what is not certain.
* C: Therefore I should not unconditionally trust my senses.

**Note on scope:** Descartes immediately limits this argument's reach. Illusions are special cases under unusual perceptual conditions (and often explicable). They do not undermine the *bulk* of perception (e.g. that I am now looking at a piece of paper). The argument therefore casts doubt on *some* sense-perceptions but is *insufficient* for global scepticism.

**Weakest premise:** P2 — that any source which has *ever* deceived is unreliable as such. We can normally identify illusions and explain them away, which suggests perception in general remains reliable.

**Examiner's attack question:** Why should occasional perceptual error generate doubt about reliable everyday perceptions like "I am looking at a piece of paper"?

---

### Wave 2 — [Argument: from Dreaming] — Descartes (Meditation I, p. 1)

* P1: When dreaming, we sometimes represent mundane things (e.g. dreaming I am looking at a piece of paper).
* P2: When dreaming, I can even have the thought "I am not dreaming" — the dream is internally indistinguishable from waking.
* P3: There is no reliable way to tell, *of my present perception*, whether I am awake or asleep.
* C1: Therefore I cannot know whether any particular perceptual experience is veridical or a dream.
* C2: Therefore *all* sense perception is doubtful — not just unusual cases.

**Important nuance — what survives the dream argument:** Even if dreaming, dreams are constructed out of basic ideas which must correspond to *something* real — Descartes says: body, extension, shape, quantity, size, motion, time. So mathematical and geometrical truths (e.g. "2 + 3 = 5") *seem* secure even within the dream argument. The dream argument therefore reaches all empirical knowledge but leaves a priori knowledge of mathematics standing.

**Weakest premise:** P3. We can object that there *are* ways of distinguishing dreams from waking — coherence, vividness, continuity with memory. Descartes anticipates this: such tests presuppose I can rely on memory to compare current perception with past dreams — but what if I am only dreaming that I remember?

**Examiner's attack question:** Why does the argument from dreaming go further than the argument from illusion? (Answer: it threatens *all* sense-perception at once, not just unusual cases.)

---

### Wave 3 — [Argument: from the Evil Demon] — Descartes (Meditation I, pp. 2–3)

* P1: It is possible that all my experiences are produced in me by an evil demon who wants to deceive me.
* P2: If this were so, even the truths of mathematics (e.g. that 2 + 3 = 5) might be wrong — the demon could deceive me whenever I reason.
* P3: If the demon is deceiving me, my experiences would be qualitatively indistinguishable from experiences I would have if no demon existed.
* C1: Therefore I cannot rule out the possibility that I am being deceived by an evil demon.
* C2: Therefore I cannot be certain even of the truths of mathematics, let alone the existence of an external world.
* C3: Therefore there is nothing I can know.

This is the move from local scepticism (about perception) to *global scepticism* (about everything, including a priori truths).

**Weakest premise:** P2 — the claim that even mathematical reasoning could be systematically corrupted by deception without detection. Descartes himself will later use the cogito to find a foothold even within this scenario.

**Examiner's attack question:** Why does the evil demon argument achieve global scepticism where the argument from dreaming did not? (Answer: it extends doubt to mathematics and reasoning, not just to sense-perception.)

---

### Why Each Wave Goes Further than the Last

| Wave | Threatens | Survives |
|---|---|---|
| Illusion | Some perceptions in unusual conditions | Most everyday perception; mathematics; reasoning |
| Dreaming | All sense-perception (since I cannot tell I am awake) | The basic ideas dreams are made of (body, extension, shape, quantity, motion, time) and so mathematics |
| Evil demon | Even mathematics and reasoning | (Nothing — until Descartes finds the cogito) |

The waves are progressively more radical. Together they generate global scepticism.

## SECTION 4 — RESPONSES TO SCEPTICISM

There are four families of response in the spec: (i) Descartes' own rationalist response; (ii) the empiricist best-explanation response (Locke, Russell, Trotter Cockburn); (iii) Berkeley's idealist response; (iv) reliabilism.

---

### Response 1 — Descartes' Own Rationalist Response

Theory — Descartes' Reconstruction of Knowledge

Core claim: We can defeat global scepticism by using rational intuition and deduction, starting from an indubitable premise (the cogito) and proving the existence of God, who guarantees that our clear and distinct ideas are reliable — restoring perception, memory, and the existence of an external world.

What this commits the thinker to: That at least one premise (the cogito) is indubitable even given the evil demon; that rational intuition can deliver synthetic a priori knowledge (existence of God, of an external physical world); that God is no deceiver.

What this rules out: That global scepticism is unanswerable; that knowledge requires no foundational rebuilding.

Key terminology:
- **Cogito** — "I think, therefore I am"; Descartes' first indubitable truth, surviving even the evil demon.
- **Clear and distinct ideas** — ideas present and accessible to the attentive mind, sharply separated from all others; the mark of certain truth.
- **Natural light** — Descartes' faculty of rational intuition.
- **Indirect realism + primary/secondary distinction** — the conclusion Descartes reaches: we can know physical objects exist and have primary qualities, but we cannot know they have secondary qualities.

The reconstruction (the order matters for spec questions):
1. **Cogito** — Even if the evil demon is deceiving me, I exist as a thinking thing (I cannot be deceived that I think; doubting is itself thinking).
2. **Clear and distinct ideas** — Reflecting on the cogito reveals that what makes it certain is its clarity and distinctness; so I can know that the truths of mathematics (which are similarly clear and distinct) are reliable.
3. **Concept of physical object** — A priori analysis reveals my concept of a physical object as something extended in space; I now have a coherent concept, but not yet knowledge of physical-object existence.
4. **God's existence** — Proven through the Trademark, cosmological, and ontological arguments using clear and distinct ideas.
5. **God is not a deceiver** — Because God is supremely perfect; deception is a defect.
6. **External world** — Because God is not a deceiver and my perceptual experiences are involuntary, my strong tendency to believe in a physical world cannot be a tendency God allows me to be deceived in. Therefore physical objects exist.
7. **Dreaming** — At the very end of the Meditations: God's non-deception licenses my reliance on memory's coherence to distinguish waking from dreaming.

The result: indirect realism with a primary/secondary distinction. Sense perception is *not* a reliable guide to the essential nature of physical objects; primary qualities track reality; secondary qualities do not.

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Does Descartes Successfully Defeat Global Scepticism?]

The objection (empiricists, Hume, others): Each of Descartes' steps is contestable. The cogito establishes only that thinking is occurring, not a persisting mental substance. The proofs of God rely on contestable a priori premises (causal-reality principle, existence as a perfection, etc.) that Hume's fork rules out as substantively unknowable a priori. Worst of all, the use of clear and distinct ideas to prove God, then the use of God to underwrite clear and distinct ideas, generates the *Cartesian Circle*.

The response (Descartes): Distinguish *while attending* to a clear and distinct idea (when its truth is forced upon me) from *not attending* (when I might wonder whether it really was true). Clear and distinct ideas are self-validating in the moment; God's role is only to underwrite *retrospective* memory of having clearly perceived something.

Who has the stronger position and why: Descartes' reconstruction is heroic but fragile. Each link in the chain is contested. Most contemporary philosophers judge the Cartesian Circle escape inadequate and the proofs of God unconvincing as a priori arguments. But the cogito itself remains a robust starting point — what it cannot do alone is build a bridge to the external world.

---

### Response 2 — Empiricist Best-Explanation Response (Locke, Russell, Trotter Cockburn)

Theory — Inference to the Best Explanation as a Reply to Scepticism

Core claim: We are not entitled to demand certainty as the standard for knowledge. The existence of mind-independent physical objects is the *best explanation* of our perceptual experience — its involuntariness, its cross-modal coherence, its systematic regularity — and that is enough to know that they exist.

What this commits the thinker to: That knowledge does not require certainty (rejecting infallibilism); that inference to the best explanation can yield knowledge.

What this rules out: Descartes' demand for indubitable foundations; the requirement that we rule out *every* logically possible alternative.

Key terminology:
- **Inference to the best explanation (IBE)** — accepting the hypothesis that best explains our evidence as known, even though it is not deductively guaranteed.
- **Indirect realism** — the metaphysical position the empiricists hold: we directly perceive sense-data, indirectly perceive mind-independent physical objects.

The argument (essentially Russell's): the regularities and continuities in my experience (the cat in the corner is later on the sofa; my sense modalities cohere systematically; my perceptions are involuntary) are best explained by the existence of mind-independent physical objects. Locke and Trotter Cockburn add the cross-modal coherence and involuntariness arguments. Together these provide an inductive case for the external world.

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Does the Best-Explanation Response Defeat Scepticism?]

The objection 1 (Descartes-style): The existence of the external world remains a *hypothesis* — it is inferred, not certainly known. Without certainty, we don't have knowledge of the external world.

The response (empiricist): Reject the demand for certainty. The Cartesian standard is too high — most of what we ordinarily call knowledge falls short of certainty. Reasonable knowledge does not require ruling out every logically possible alternative.

The objection 2 (the brain-in-vat parity objection): If I were a brain in a vat, my sense experience would be exactly as it is now — equally involuntary, coherent, and systematic. The brain-in-vat hypothesis explains my experience just as well as the physical-object hypothesis. So inference to the best explanation cannot prefer physical objects over the sceptical alternative. Therefore IBE fails to meet global scepticism.

The response (empiricist, attempted): Appeal to simplicity, to the fit with science, or to the continuity-of-experience features. None of these decisively rules out the brain-in-vat scenario.

Who has the stronger position and why: The empiricist response handles *local* scepticism well — it gives us a non-Cartesian story about how we know there's an external world. But against *global* scepticism it stumbles on the parity objection: the brain-in-vat scenario explains the same data. So IBE replies to scepticism are at best partial.

---

### Response 3 — Berkeley's Idealist Response

Theory — Berkeley's Anti-Sceptical Idealism

Core claim: Scepticism only arises if we draw a distinction between appearance and reality. Reject that distinction (deny mind-independent matter), and reality just is what we experience — making sceptical doubts about whether appearance matches reality vacuous.

What this commits the thinker to: That all that exists are minds and ideas; that reality is constituted by our perceptions (which God wills); that the very idea of mind-independent matter is incoherent.

What this rules out: Realism; the view that there is a "real world" hidden behind the appearances.

Key terminology:
- **Esse est percipi (aut percipere)** — to be is to be perceived (or to perceive). The slogan of Berkeley's idealism.
- **Appearance/reality distinction** — the realist's claim that what exists may differ from what we perceive. Berkeley denies this.

How this answers scepticism: Scepticism asks "How do we know that what we experience corresponds to mind-independent reality?" Berkeley's answer: there is no mind-independent reality; what we experience *is* reality. The question simply doesn't arise.

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Does Berkeley's Idealism Defeat Scepticism?]

The objection (the brain-in-vat parallel): Berkeley's metaphysics looks suspiciously similar to the brain-in-vat scenario. He says my experiences are caused by another mind (God) outside my mind. The brain-in-vat scenario says my experiences are caused by a physical computer outside my mind. Why God rather than supercomputer?

The response (Berkeley): A supercomputer is a *physical object* (and so is a brain). Berkeley has independently argued that mind-independent physical objects cannot exist coherently. Therefore the brain-in-vat scenario describes something incoherent — it cannot even get off the ground.

The further objection: Berkeley's anti-sceptical move is a Pyrrhic victory — it dissolves scepticism only by rejecting the existence of mind-independent reality, which is itself wildly counter-intuitive. The cure is worse than the disease.

Who has the stronger position and why: Berkeley genuinely sidesteps scepticism — if his idealism is correct, the global sceptical challenge cannot get traction. But the cost (denying matter, requiring God to perceive everything continuously) is so high that few find it worth paying. The deeper issue: even if we accept Berkeley's idealism, *local* sceptical doubts remain (about other minds, the past, etc.).

---

### Response 4 — Reliabilism

Theory — Reliabilism as a Reply to Scepticism

Core claim: Knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable cognitive process; justification is not required. To know that there are physical objects, I do not need to know that I am not a brain in a vat — I only need my belief-forming process actually to be reliable.

What this commits the thinker to: That knowledge does not require subjective access to one's justification; that being in fact reliable suffices for knowledge (an "externalist" view); that animals and young children can have knowledge without sophisticated reasoning about justification.

What this rules out: The internalist assumption shared by Descartes and the empiricist responses — that the knower must be able to *show* their belief is justified. Also rules out the equivalence of "I know that p" and "I know that I know that p."

Key terminology:
- **Reliable process** — a cognitive process (e.g. perception in normal conditions) that tends to produce mostly true beliefs.
- **Externalism** — the view that what makes a belief knowledge can be (partly) outside the knower's awareness — what matters is the actual reliability of the process, not the knower's grasp of it.
- **KK-thesis** — the (rejected) claim that knowing that p requires knowing that one knows that p.

How this answers scepticism: The sceptic says "you don't know there are physical objects unless you can rule out the brain-in-vat scenario." The reliabilist replies: this confuses *knowing that p* with *knowing that I know that p*. If perception is in fact reliable (and I am not a brain in a vat), then I know that there are physical objects — even if I cannot prove the brain-in-vat scenario false.

---

### [Argument: The Reliabilist Reply] — Goldman-style reliabilism applied to scepticism

* P1: Knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable cognitive process.
* P2: If I am not a brain in a vat, perception is a reliable process and most of my perceptual beliefs are true.
* P3: To know that p, I do not need also to know that I know that p.
* P4: The sceptic confuses these two — demanding that I rule out the brain-in-vat scenario as a condition on first-order knowledge that there are physical objects.
* C1: Therefore even if I cannot rule out the brain-in-vat scenario, I can still have knowledge of the physical world (provided I am in fact not a brain in a vat).
* C2: The sceptical challenge fails as an attack on first-order knowledge.

**Weakest premise:** The reliabilist analysis of knowledge itself (P1). Critics argue reliabilism is vulnerable to its own counterexamples (e.g. Gettier-style cases, the "evil demon" case where my belief-forming process *seems* reliable from the inside but isn't, the "lottery" cases).

**Examiner's attack question:** Doesn't reliabilism just sidestep the sceptical challenge rather than meet it? The sceptic asks how I know perception is reliable; the reliabilist replies that I don't need to know — but isn't this changing the subject?

---

### [Topic of Dispute: Does Reliabilism Defeat Scepticism?]

The objection (the internalist): If I cannot rule out the brain-in-vat scenario, then I have no good reason to think perception is reliable. Reliabilism allows knowledge without the knower having any reason to think they know — which is hardly satisfactory. The objection presses: surely I need to know that I know.

The response (reliabilist): The KK-thesis (that knowing p requires knowing that I know p) is false. The cognitive process that produces my first-order belief about the world (perception) can be reliable even if my higher-order cognitive process for forming beliefs about whether *that* process is reliable is itself unreliable. Animals know things via perception even though they cannot reflect on whether their perceptions are reliable. So I can know that there are physical objects without knowing that I know.

Who has the stronger position and why: The reliabilist response is the most successful answer to global scepticism *if* we accept externalism about knowledge. It does not refute the sceptic on the sceptic's own (internalist) terms — it refuses to play the internalist game. Whether this is a victory or a sidestep depends on which theory of knowledge you find more plausible. Many find reliabilism unsatisfying as a response to the sceptic precisely because it ignores the sceptic's question rather than answering it.

## SECTION 5 — KEY DIRECT DIALOGUES

### Descartes vs Hume — On Whether Scepticism Can Be Defeated by Reason

**Descartes' position:** Yes — the cogito is indubitable, clear and distinct ideas are self-validating when attended to, and a priori reasoning can prove the existence of God, whose non-deception underwrites the rest of our knowledge.

**Hume's position:** No — Hume's fork blocks Descartes' a priori proofs (the causal-reality principle, the impossibility of infinite regress, existence as a perfection are neither analytic nor knowable a posteriori). Substantive a priori knowledge of synthetic propositions about the external world is impossible. Knowledge of matters of fact comes only from experience and induction.

**The crux of the disagreement:** Whether reason alone can yield substantive knowledge of the external world, or whether all knowledge of contingent reality must be empirical.

**Who has the stronger argument and why:** Hume's fork is a powerful methodological critique that exposes the unargued-for premises in Descartes' proofs. But Hume pays a price — he leaves us with an inductive account of knowledge that itself struggles against global scepticism (since induction cannot deliver certainty either, and the brain-in-vat parity objection applies). The dispute is a stalemate at the level of ambition: Descartes promises too much; Hume's account, while more disciplined, also leaves global scepticism standing.

---

### Descartes vs Reliabilism — On What Knowledge Requires

**Descartes' position:** Knowledge requires certainty — beliefs must be indubitable, founded on clear and distinct ideas. To know that there are physical objects, I must be able to *prove* (with certainty) that I am not deceived.

**Reliabilism's position:** Knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable cognitive process. The knower does not need certainty; does not need to *prove* the absence of deception; does not need second-order knowledge that they know.

**The crux of the disagreement:** Whether knowledge is internalist (the knower must have access to what makes their belief knowledge) or externalist (it suffices that the belief is in fact produced by a reliable process).

**Who has the stronger argument and why:** Reliabilism better fits ordinary attributions of knowledge (to children, animals, busy adults who don't reflect on epistemology). Descartes' standard, while methodologically powerful, makes most of what we call knowledge fail to qualify. But reliabilism may be answering a different question than the sceptic asks — it explains what knowledge *is*, not why we should be confident we *have* it. The contest is partly about what philosophical scepticism is *for*: is it a threat to be answered on its own terms, or a misconception to be diagnosed and dismissed?

---

### Empiricist Best-Explanation vs Berkeley — On What Best Explains Experience

**Empiricist position (Locke/Russell/Trotter Cockburn):** The best explanation of involuntary, coherent, systematic perceptual experience is the existence of mind-independent physical objects causing it.

**Berkeley's position:** The best explanation is that my experiences are caused by another mind (God) willing ideas in me. This explanation does the same work without the (incoherent) postulation of mind-independent matter.

**The crux of the disagreement:** Whether "the best explanation" of perceptual regularity is realist (physical objects) or idealist (God's willing).

**Who has the stronger argument and why:** Berkeley's reply is internally consistent and avoids the problem of mind-matter interaction. But it requires accepting a substantial theological commitment, and his arguments for the incoherence of mind-independent matter (especially the master argument) are widely judged unsuccessful. The realist explanation is more parsimonious within a naturalistic framework. Net: realist IBE is more credible *if* one accepts that mind-independent matter is coherent — which is precisely the question Berkeley pressed.

## SECTION 6 — OBJECTIONS THAT TARGET ALL RESPONSES

### The Brain-in-Vat Parity Objection

This is the master objection that cuts across most responses to scepticism (especially the empiricist IBE response).

**Statement:** If I were a brain in a vat, my experience would be qualitatively identical to my experience as I am. The brain-in-vat hypothesis explains all my evidence equally well. Therefore no inference from experience can prefer the realist hypothesis over the sceptical one.

**Which responses it threatens:**
- *Empiricist IBE* — directly defeated, as IBE cannot prefer one of two equally explanatory hypotheses.
- *Descartes' rationalism* — partially evaded (Descartes claims rational intuition gives us knowledge independent of experience), but only if his proofs of God succeed (which Hume contests).
- *Berkeley's idealism* — sidestepped (Berkeley denies mind-independent matter, so the brain-in-vat scenario is incoherent for him).
- *Reliabilism* — sidestepped (reliabilism doesn't require ruling out the scenario; it requires the process to *in fact* be reliable).

The objection's deep force: subjective indistinguishability seems to undermine any internalist response that proceeds from experience.

---

### The Self-Defeat Worry (Anti-Scepticism)

Sometimes pressed against scepticism itself rather than against responses.

**Statement:** "Nothing is known" — if asserted as a *theory* — is self-defeating. To know that nothing is known is to know something. Therefore scepticism-as-theory cannot be coherently held.

**The standard sceptical reply:** Scepticism is best understood not as a theory ("nothing is known") but as a *challenge* ("rule out these alternatives, or admit you don't know"). The challenge is not self-defeating; it does not require the sceptic to claim knowledge of anything.

This reply is why textbooks treat philosophical scepticism as a *methodological challenge*, not a doctrine.

## SECTION 7 — COMPARATIVE SUMMARY TABLE

| Thinker / Position | Position | Core Claim | Strongest Argument For | Strongest Objection Against | How They Respond |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Philosophical sceptic (Cartesian-style)** | Global scepticism | Our usual justifications for knowledge are inadequate to rule out sceptical alternatives like the brain-in-vat or evil demon. | The brain-in-vat thought experiment + the three waves of doubt — together they show that no source of belief (perception, memory, reasoning) is immune to subjective indistinguishability from a deceptive scenario. | Self-defeat worry: "I know that nothing is known" is contradictory; if scepticism is asserted as a doctrine it refutes itself. | Reframe scepticism as a methodological *challenge* rather than a doctrine — it asks us to rule out alternatives, it does not assert we cannot. This is non-self-defeating. |
| **Descartes** | Rationalist anti-scepticism (with indirect realism + primary/secondary distinction) | Global scepticism is defeated by rational intuition and deduction starting from the cogito and reaching God's existence and non-deception. | The cogito — "I think therefore I am" — is indubitable even given the evil demon, providing a foundation for reconstruction. | Cartesian Circle (clear-and-distinct ideas validate God; God validates clear-and-distinct ideas); Hume's fork blocks the a priori proofs of God. | Distinguish "while attending" to a clear and distinct idea (truth is forced upon me) from "after attending" (when memory needs God's underwriting to be reliable). The proofs of God do not depend on any prior reliability of clear-and-distinct ideas — only on attending to them. |
| **Locke / Russell / Trotter Cockburn** | Empiricist anti-scepticism (best-explanation indirect realism) | The existence of mind-independent physical objects is the best explanation of involuntary, coherent, systematic perceptual experience. | Cross-modal coherence + involuntariness of perception + the systematic continuity of experience over time (Russell's cat) all best explained by mind-independent physical objects. | Brain-in-vat parity objection — the sceptical scenario explains our experience just as well, so IBE cannot prefer the realist hypothesis. Also: this does not deliver certainty. | Reject the Cartesian demand for certainty (knowledge does not require it). Concede that against *global* scepticism IBE is limited; argue that for ordinary epistemological purposes, IBE is sufficient. |
| **Berkeley** | Idealist anti-scepticism | Reject the appearance/reality distinction; reality just is what we perceive (ideas willed by God). The very framing of the sceptical challenge presupposes incoherent mind-independent matter. | Three arguments against mind-independent matter (substratum incoherence; experience supports idealism; realism implies scepticism) — together undermining the framework that lets scepticism arise. | The position is wildly counter-intuitive (matter doesn't exist?); the master argument is a use-mention fallacy; Berkeley's God plays a role suspiciously similar to a brain-in-vat supercomputer. | The supercomputer is a physical object — and physical objects (mind-independent matter) cannot coherently exist. Therefore the brain-in-vat scenario is incoherent. The God-explanation, by contrast, posits only minds and ideas, which we already know exist. |
| **Reliabilist** | Externalist anti-scepticism | Knowledge is true belief from a reliable process; the knower needs no internal access to justification. To know there are physical objects, I do not need to know I am not a brain in a vat. | Animals and young children plainly have knowledge through perception without reflective justification — reliabilism explains this; internalist accounts struggle to. The KK-thesis (knowing entails knowing-that-one-knows) is implausible. | Reliabilism evades rather than answers the sceptic; if I cannot tell whether I am a brain in a vat, I have no reason to think my processes are reliable. The reply just changes the subject. | Distinguish first-order from higher-order knowledge: I can know p without knowing that I know p. Whether the sceptic finds this "satisfying" is irrelevant; what matters is whether the analysis of knowledge is correct. The cognitive process producing my belief about p can be reliable while my process for evaluating it is not. |
| **Hume (background figure)** | Empiricist anti-rationalism | All knowledge is either of relations of ideas (analytic, a priori) or matters of fact (synthetic, a posteriori, learned via causal inference). Hume's fork blocks Descartes' a priori proofs. | Hume's fork — substantive existence claims (about God, external world) cannot be analytic; therefore they require empirical evidence which experience cannot guarantee. | Hume's own account leaves global scepticism standing — induction cannot deliver certainty either; the brain-in-vat parity objection applies. | Concede that strict certainty is unavailable, but argue that within naturalised epistemology, custom and habit (rather than reason) ground our beliefs, which are sufficient for practice if not for armchair refutation of the sceptic. |
